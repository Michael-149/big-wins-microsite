## What we mean by social science

By social science we mean research on how people, organizations and societies behave, and on how rules, markets and institutions shape that behavior. It spans economics, psychology, sociology, political science and related fields; in these eight cases it is mostly economics, with psychology, sociology and public health research on behavior and programs. Some cases sit on a boundary: the climate-economy model joins economics to climate science, and the syringe services research drew on epidemiology. We included them because the contribution we describe is the social science part.

## What counts as a case

A case, for us, is a change that has already happened (we look back, not forward) and that meets two tests. It reached large numbers of people, went deep for the people it reached, or helped people who are especially vulnerable. And published evidence shows that social science research played a substantial, documented part in it.

We judge a case on its scale and on the role research played, not on whether everyone agrees the change was good. Reasonable people disagree about some of the policies here, and about some of the candidates we considered.

Large, traceable changes are only one kind of success. Most useful social science improves a single program, informs a local decision or builds knowledge that others use years later, and the changes described here rest on many such contributions. A collection of large cases is not a claim that smaller or slower work matters less.

## How we chose the eight

Between 2021 and 2025, Stanford Impact Labs (SIL) kept a running list of examples where social science had reached large numbers of people. We asked more than 30 people for suggestions, among them Stanford faculty and SIL staff, and sought out suggestions that would widen the range of disciplines. A 2023 workshop hosted at Stanford, with social scientists from several universities, added more. So did the proof-points analysis of the Public Solutions R&D network, a set of short case summaries that supplied 30 of the candidates.

The result was a long list of 67 candidates. We then applied six criteria:

1. **Large societal benefit.** Many people benefited, the benefit was deep, or it reached people who are especially vulnerable.
2. **Clear attribution.** Evidence that social science research played a major part in the change at scale.
3. **Well-documented pathway.** Credible published sources trace the path from research to change.
4. **Diversity of domains, geographies and disciplines.** A mix of subjects (such as health, education, environment and economic mobility), of places, and of disciplines.
5. **Variety of pathways to impact.** Cases where research shaped policy, created a market, drove the expansion of a program or shifted public norms.
6. **Variety of sources of ideas.** Good ideas come from many places: from the communities affected by a problem, from practitioners and from researchers. Research can speed a promising idea to scale, or save effort on one that does not work. We wanted cases from each source.

We read the second criterion as a documented, substantial contribution from research, not proof that research was decisive; in several cases other forces mattered as much or more, and the case pages say so. Growth mindset meets the first criterion least well: [its page](/case-studies/growth-mindset) reports a small, contested benefit for lower-achieving students and no documented savings. We kept it as a contrast, because it shows most clearly how an idea can spread in forms its tests did not cover.

The sixth criterion is why we added syringe services programs, which were not among the 67 candidates: they began outside research, and researchers then tested them. Strong candidates we did not choose this time include conditional cash transfers, Teaching at the Right Level and school choice assignment systems.

The Cases we considered page shows the long list. We merged duplicates, removed items outside social science or without a documented research contribution, and added two candidates that our source notes named but the list missed, syringe services programs among them. That leaves 49 entries, including the eight featured here. Only the eight have been fact-checked in full.

## What our choices favored

Two working priorities shaped the final eight, beyond the six criteria. First, we favored cases with relatively short causal chains, where the line from research to change runs through a few documented steps. Second, we favored pathways unlike the deep, embedded research partnerships SIL often funds, in which researchers work closely with one or a few partners over years to design, test and scale a solution. We made both choices to learn as much as we could from routes we knew less well.

Those priorities explain much of the set's shape. It leans toward economics, policy and market design, where research often enters through a rule, an auction, a contract or a model. It underrepresents slow shifts in how a field or the public understands a problem, and it underrepresents partnership-based work, which is where much social science creates value. Read the eight as a sample built for learning, not as a representative picture of how social science does good.

We did not choose cases for any Stanford connection, and none of the eight is an SIL project. Four have Stanford ties, which their pages disclose: [growth mindset](/case-studies/growth-mindset), the [spectrum auctions](/case-studies/fcc-spectrum), the [vaccine commitment](/case-studies/amc-vaccines) and [Moving to Opportunity](/case-studies/moving-to-opportunity). In the other four, Stanford appears at most as the home of one study or data source the page cites.

## How we drew the lessons

Each case began as desk research by a member of the SIL team, using process tracing: following the sequence of events from the research to the change and checking each link against the evidence. In fall 2025 the team compared the drafts side by side and wrote down patterns that appeared in several cases.

Reviewers' comments on early drafts then reshaped the lessons. We rewrote each lesson as a claim that could turn out to be wrong, tied each one to every case that illustrates it, strongest first, and added a section on where it may not hold.

The lessons are our reading of eight cases, not the output of a systematic analysis. We did not code the cases against a fixed framework, and a different team could reasonably draw different lessons from the same material. Each lesson page names the cases behind it so readers can judge for themselves.

## Limits to keep in mind

- **No failed efforts.** We chose cases in which research helped change policy or practice. Efforts that followed the same strategies and failed are not in the sample, so these cases cannot show that any lesson causes success. One global health collection has paired its successes with programs that fell short when scaled up {{cite:glassman-temin2016}}; we have not yet done that.
- **Eight cases.** A sample this small can suggest patterns. It cannot estimate how often a pattern holds.
- **Hindsight.** Looking back makes a path look clearer than it was. Features that seem decisive now may not have been visible, or predictable, at the time.
- **Attribution.** Research was one force among many in every case, and some changes might have happened without it. We use words like "informed" and "helped make the case for" unless the evidence shows more.
- **Public sources only.** We worked from published studies, reports and histories. Published accounts tend to favor success stories and the views of those who wrote them, and they leave out much of what happened in private.
- **A narrow spread.** We aimed for a mix of places and disciplines but fell short. Seven of the eight cases involve policy or programs in the United States, only one (the pneumococcal vaccine commitment) centers on low- and middle-income countries, and economics is central to most of them.
- **What worked once may not transfer.** Political, institutional and cultural conditions differ across places and decades. A route that worked in one setting is evidence, not a blueprint.
- **Our outside view.** The SIL team wrote these cases as outsiders to most of these fields, and none of us took part in these stories. People who were there, or who study these fields, will see things we missed or got wrong.
- **Not new.** Reviewers of early drafts pointed out that several lessons echo long-standing findings in research on how evidence is used and how policy changes. They are right: each lesson page names the earlier work it echoes, and the next section describes the main strands. [Lesson 1](/lessons/1-public-engagement), for example, echoes a review that found collaboration and better relationships between researchers and policymakers were the facilitators of evidence use reported most often {{cite:oliver2014}}; [lesson 6](/lessons/6-policy-windows) echoes the classic account of policy windows {{cite:kingdon1984}}; and [lesson 8](/lessons/8-different-roles) echoes the classic distinction among several meanings of research use {{cite:weiss1979}}. What we hope to add is the evidence and the stories, set side by side.

## What this builds on

Much of what these cases show has been documented before. The work we found most useful falls into five groups.

**How research gets used.** Carol Weiss described research use as several different things, from answering a question a decision maker already has to what she called enlightenment, the slow process by which research changes how people think about a problem {{cite:weiss1979}}. Sandra Nutley, Isabel Walter and Huw Davies brought together the evidence on research use in public services, and pointed to the links between researchers and users, and the context in which research arrives, as much as to the research itself {{cite:nutley2007}}. Vivian Tseng of the William T. Grant Foundation described how policymakers and practitioners acquire research, often through trusted peers and intermediaries, and how they must interpret it for their own circumstances, since research does not speak for itself {{cite:tseng2012}}. A white paper for the foundation by Cynthia Coburn, William Penuel and Kimberly Geil defines research-practice partnerships as long-term collaborations in which researchers and school district staff study problems of practice, and solutions to them, together {{cite:coburn2013}}.

**What helps and what gets in the way.** An updated systematic review by Kathryn Oliver and colleagues, which added 145 studies, found that the barriers to policymakers' use of evidence reported most often were poor access to good, relevant research and research that arrives too late, and that the facilitators reported most often were collaboration and better relationships between researchers and policymakers {{cite:oliver2014}}. A later review by Oliver and Paul Cairney collected the advice given to academics who want to influence policy, from understanding policy processes to being entrepreneurial or finding someone who is, and warned that this advice masks real disagreements and could mislead newcomers unless it is read alongside the peer-reviewed research {{cite:oliver-cairney2019}}. A study of five research projects in low- and middle-income countries by Séverine Erismann and colleagues found that involving stakeholders, including policymakers, from the start of a project made it more likely that the research would inform policy, especially where the policy environment was supportive and stable {{cite:erismann2021}}.

**How policy changes.** John Kingdon's account of agenda setting describes separate streams of problems, policy ideas and politics, and argues that change becomes likely when they come together at moments he called policy windows {{cite:kingdon1984}}. A guide by Sarah Stachowiak of ORS Impact sets out Kingdon's theory alongside nine others, such as advocacy coalitions and the diffusion of innovations, for advocates, funders and evaluators planning policy change work {{cite:stachowiak2013}}.

**What happens at scale.** Stefano DellaVigna and Elizabeth Linos assembled 126 randomized trials (experiments that use a lottery to decide who gets a program) run by two of the largest nudge units in the United States, teams that test changes to how programs are designed and communicated. They compared these trials, which covered 23 million people, with nudge trials published in academic journals. The nudge units' average effect on take-up (the share of people who took the action encouraged, such as signing up) was 1.4 percentage points, against 8.7 in the published trials, and the authors attribute about 70 percent of that gap to selective publication made worse by small samples {{cite:dellavigna-linos2022}}. John List's book on scaling sets out the conditions an idea must meet to keep its effect when it grows {{cite:list2022}}, and Esther Duflo argues that economists who help design policy need to engage with its details, as a plumber would {{cite:duflo2017}}.

**Collections of success cases.** The Center for Global Development's Millions Saved documented 17 large health programs in developing countries that worked {{cite:levine2004}}. Its 2016 edition profiled 22 programs, including four that fell short of their health targets when scaled up {{cite:glassman-temin2016}}. Exemplars in Global Health studies positive outlier countries, those that made unusual progress on a health problem, to learn what they did {{cite:carter2020}}. We borrowed the idea of a growing, shared catalog from both, and we have more to learn from their methods.

## How we researched and checked the facts

The cases rely on desk research: published studies, government and agency reports, the websites of organizations involved, and credible books and journalism for narrative history. We triangulated, checking each key point against more than one independent source where we could. This release does not draw on interviews.

For this release we rebuilt every page around its sources. For each case and lesson we listed every factual claim (each number, date, name, attribution and statement about current status) and checked it against a primary source where one exists: the study itself, the official report or the agency's own page. A second, independent pass then checked each claim against the source it cites, and we corrected or cut what did not hold up.

Estimates carry the period they cover; where credible sources disagree, we say so or use the more conservative figure. Statements about current status are as of November 2026.

Artificial intelligence (AI) tools assisted with drafting and fact-checking. Every factual claim in the cases links to a published source; the lessons cite other work directly and link to the case pages for case facts.

Each page lists its sources, and numbered notes in the text tie each claim to them. Stanford Impact Labs is responsible for any errors that remain.
