## In the cases

**Housing agencies helped build the program.** Seattle and King County's housing authorities developed Creating Moves to Opportunity (CMTO) with the researchers, whose 2018 to 2020 trial enrolled 712 families with vouchers ([Moving to Opportunity](/case-studies/moving-to-opportunity)). With CMTO's help, 53% of families moved to high-opportunity areas, where low-income children have tended to do better as adults, against 15% in the control group. Interviews by Johns Hopkins sociologist Stefanie DeLuca's team suggested why full services had five times the effect of information and money alone: they met each family's needs, from finding units to brokering with landlords.

**A program director coauthored the evaluation.** New Haven's city-run [needle exchange](/case-studies/needle-exchange) had its evaluation built into the program, and its director, Elaine O'Keefe, coauthored the main paper. Because researchers judged it unethical to test clients, Yale operations researcher Edward Kaplan, who builds mathematical models for decisions, proposed testing the returned needles, which also kept clients anonymous.

**Economists and donors designed it together.** From 2003 to 2005, economist Michael Kremer, then at Harvard, co-chaired a Gates-funded working group at the Center for Global Development, a think tank, that turned the [advance market commitment](/case-studies/amc-vaccines) (a donor promise to help pay for new vaccines once they exist) into a proposal. After pneumococcal disease was chosen for a pilot, an Economic Expert Group the donors formed in May 2007 recommended tying the money to firms' long-term supply commitments, and four of its members helped the donors and agency staff set the price.

**An agency hired its own economist.** After Congress authorized spectrum auctions in 1993 and gave the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) 210 days to write rules, the agency hired economist John McMillan of the University of California, San Diego ([spectrum auctions](/case-studies/fcc-spectrum)). Caltech experimental economist Charles Plott tested candidate rules in the laboratory during the rulemaking.

## The pattern

Close work with decision makers, meaning work with those deciding on or running a change while it was designed or tested, appeared in six of the eight cases, for years in the vaccine commitment (2003 to 2008) and through CMTO's 2018 to 2020 trial. Three ran through standing arrangements, programs or labs with research built in: CMTO, New Haven's exchange and a [growth mindset](/case-studies/growth-mindset) session that World Bank and Peruvian researchers built with Peru's education ministry through its innovation lab, MineduLAB {{cite:jpal-minedulab}}.

Most of it shaped how a decision was carried out more than whether, the order political scientist John Kingdon of the University of Michigan describes: agenda setters turn to specialists, researchers among them, for alternatives {{cite:kingdon2014}}. Donors had committed $1.5 billion before forming the expert group, though the think tank's blueprint came first. In [emissions trading](/case-studies/emissions-trading), George H. W. Bush had made a campaign pledge on acid rain before his incoming counsel, C. Boyden Gray, brought Harvard economist Robert Stavins, whose Project 88 study proposed trading, and the head of the Environmental Defense Fund to the White House, where the Fund's staff worked on the first trading proposal.

Two cases cut the other way. Federal staff ran Yale economist William Nordhaus's [Dynamic Integrated Climate-Economy (DICE) model](/case-studies/dice-modeling) from public code to help set the government's 2010 social cost of carbon (the future damage from one more ton of carbon dioxide, discounted to today's dollars), and we found no record of his working on it. In [retirement savings defaults](/case-studies/behavioral-nudges), employers and the Internal Revenue Service acted before the main studies; the first, coauthored with a corporate compensation executive, analyzed a switch already made.

By the people affected we mean the individuals a program serves or a rule reaches, such as tenants, people who inject drugs and students. Organized interests were another matter: bidding firms paid most academic economists in the FCC's first rulemaking.

Researchers learned from those individuals in two cases. In Moving to Opportunity, interviews with families in Boston led economist Jeffrey Kling, then at Princeton, and two coauthors to refocus their data collection on safety and health, where their next results found the largest effects {{cite:kling2001}}. In growth mindset, researchers revised the online sessions behind the national study after focus groups and interviews with high school students {{cite:yeager2016}}. We found no case in which the people affected shared decisions with researchers; in needle exchange the order ran the other way, as a drug users' group and activists started exchanges and research followed.

## Why it is easy to miss

A funder tracking studies would see little of this work, often paid for outside any study: by a foundation for the vaccine working group and by bidders in the FCC case. One looking only for standing arrangements would have missed half of it. The people affected are harder still to see: in published results they mostly appear as data, such as tax records or returned syringes.

## What it means for funders and research leaders

- **Fund the capacity to answer when decision makers ask.** The FCC, a White House counsel and donors each asked first. Pay for researchers' time to answer such requests on a decision maker's timetable, as well as for new studies.
- **Pay for engagement with a named task, or someone with a stake will.** Tie the money to a task, such as designing services or setting a price.
- **Fund listening before the design is fixed.** Interviews with families changed what Moving to Opportunity measured, and student feedback helped revise the growth mindset sessions. Budget fieldwork early, and ask what it may change.
- **Make interests visible.** Ask researchers to disclose who pays for their engagement. Where designers also evaluate, as in CMTO, guard the evaluation: MDRC, a research nonprofit, ran CMTO's trial under an analysis plan registered in advance.
- **A question for your own projects:** who outside the research team will act on this work, who asked for it, and how can the people it affects change it?

## Where it may not hold

We chose only cases in which change happened, so close work may be as common in efforts that changed nothing. Elsewhere it was often not enough: in 73 trials that 30 US cities co-designed with a national nudge unit (a team applying behavioral science for governments), cities adopted the tested change 27% of the time {{cite:dellavigna2024,dellavigna2022wp}}.

The eight were chosen to underweight the deep research partnerships we often fund, so the lesson cannot say partnerships matter less, only that close work appeared without them. The same choice may underweight work with the people affected, and our public sources record working relationships poorly.

Who pays for engagement can bend advice. McMillan and two coauthors, among them economist Preston McAfee, a bidders' adviser then at the University of Texas at Austin, say academic economists generally would not defend clients' goals that conflicted with the social interest; Roanoke College economist Edward Nik-Khah argues such accounts omit how firms' funding shaped economists' participation. Closeness has other costs: possible selective use of evidence {{cite:innvaer2002}}, and risks for researchers, their partners and scholarship that advocates of coproduction (research done jointly with its users) rarely count {{cite:oliver2019}}.

## What others have found

This lesson's first half is familiar. Sociologist Carol Weiss of Harvard described research used interactively, as one input in long exchanges among people working on a problem {{cite:weiss1979}}, and reviews find personal contact and collaboration reported most often as helping policymakers use research {{cite:innvaer2002,oliver2014}}. But the earlier review's authors judged that such studies give "only limited support for commonly held beliefs" about what helps {{cite:innvaer2002}}, and a review of 36 reviews found that "unstructured interaction and collaboration between decision-makers and researchers tended to have a lower likelihood of success" {{cite:langer2016}}.

A 2021 William T. Grant Foundation report defines research-practice partnerships in education as long-term collaborations organized to "ensure that all partners have a say in the joint work," whose partners can include families and youth {{cite:farrell2021}}, though a 2016 review found studies of them "are few" {{cite:coburn-penuel2016}}. Community-based research in public health asks for "active involvement of community members, organizational representatives, and researchers in all aspects of the research process" {{cite:israel1998}}. On the record we found, none of the eight meets that standard.
