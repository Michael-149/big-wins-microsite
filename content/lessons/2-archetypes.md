## In the cases

**Path one: an open model that others ran.** Yale economist William Nordhaus began modeling carbon dioxide limits in 1974 and published his open [DICE (Dynamic Integrated Climate-Economy) model](/case-studies/dice-modeling) in 1992. For the 2010 federal social cost of carbon (the future damage from one more ton of carbon dioxide, discounted to today's dollars), government staff ran DICE and two other teams' models with inputs they chose. We found no record that Nordhaus worked on the estimate, which he criticized in 2011.

**Path one: an old idea, a new economic case.** The World Health Organization discussed promising in advance to buy vaccines for poor countries in 1996; in 2000 economist Michael Kremer, then at Harvard, [set out its economic case](/case-studies/amc-vaccines) and carried it through a Gates-funded working group he co-chaired. Finance ministries gave it political backing, and in February 2007 five governments and the Gates Foundation committed $1.5 billion; the donors' expert group then recommended tying the money to firms' long-term supply commitments.

**Path two: many studies, alongside reviews.** Drug users, activists, clinicians and governments started [needle exchanges](/case-studies/needle-exchange); researchers measured them in New Haven, New York City and Britain, and from 1993 to 1997 four reviews for Congress and federal agencies weighed the evidence. In April 1998 Health and Human Services (HHS) Secretary Donna Shalala determined that exchanges can help reduce HIV transmission without encouraging drug use, but the administration kept the federal funding ban.

**Path two: research that decision makers drew on.** [Moving to Opportunity (MTO)](/case-studies/moving-to-opportunity) assigned 4,604 families by lottery from 1994 to 1998, and two federal evaluations found no gains in adults' earnings. In 2016, economists Raj Chetty, Nathaniel Hendren and Lawrence Katz, now at Harvard and MIT, found that children whose families used the experimental voucher to move before age 13 earned 31% more in their mid-twenties than children in the control group, an effect on movers. The Department of Housing and Urban Development's 2020 notice for the mobility demonstration Congress created in 2019 cites the MTO research.

## The pattern

We sorted the cases by what decision makers took up. In path one it was a usable tool from a researcher who stayed with the question for a decade or more, and others did much of the carrying. In path two it was findings from many teams: a study, a body of studies or a proposal built from them.

[Growth mindset](/case-studies/growth-mindset) is path one only in part. Psychologist Carol Dweck, now at Stanford, pursued the research behind it from 1973, but PERTS (Project for Education Research That Scales), co-founded by Stanford doctoral graduate Dave Paunesku, built the online sessions, and psychologist David Yeager of the University of Texas at Austin led the 2019 national study.

Path two includes Project 88, directed by Harvard Kennedy School economist Robert Stavins, which turned decades of [emissions trading](/case-studies/emissions-trading) economics into a proposal to cap emissions and let firms trade permits; President George H. W. Bush commended it in June 1989. A citation can also back a position already taken {{cite:weiss1979}}: Treasury's July 2000 fact sheet cited studies of [automatic enrollment](/case-studies/behavioral-nudges) in retirement plans two years after the Internal Revenue Service (IRS) had approved the practice.

Automatic enrollment mixed the paths: economist Richard Thaler, now at the University of Chicago, proposed it in 1994, but employers and the IRS acted first and other teams produced the evidence. [Spectrum auctions](/case-studies/fcc-spectrum) fit neither: Federal Communications Commission staff led by Evan Kwerel, an economist who had proposed auctions with a colleague in 1985, built the format largely from two independent proposals, by Stanford economists Paul Milgrom and Robert Wilson and by economist Preston McAfee, then at the University of Texas at Austin.

## Why it is easy to miss

Path one is easy to read as a great researcher's story. Yet Harvard Kennedy School economists Joseph Aldy, a White House adviser in 2009 and 2010, and Stavins, who directed Project 88, find only one significant case of Nordhaus's research shaping policy directly, the federal working group's use of DICE, and call that route "considerably less frequent than commonly assumed" {{cite:aldystavins2020}}. His larger influence, they judge, was indirect, through ideas such as carbon taxes and clubs of countries as well as others' use of DICE {{cite:aldystavins2020}}.

Path two is hard to see while it happens, because no single study looks decisive. Reviews and proposals often made the studies usable, each for a particular audience: the needle exchange reviews were for Congress and federal agencies, two senators created Project 88, and a 2005 brief proposing an automatic 401(k) asked Congress for a change in law.

## What it means for funders and research leaders

- **Back persistence that leaves something others can use, and pay for the handoff.** Nordhaus kept DICE open and updated, yet the Environmental Protection Agency's 2023 estimate used newer models built by others. Budget for documentation, updates and a partner who can deliver the tool without its author. Research leaders can credit such tools in promotion.
- **Pay for independent tests of tools and practices already in use.** In growth mindset, trials run or reported by the programs' developers found gains, while independent evaluations found smaller gains or none. Employers adopted automatic enrollment before it was studied, and the first study showed most new savers staying at a low 3% default. Fund analysis plans registered in advance and independent re-analyses, as the 2019 national study had.
- **Fund syntheses written for a particular decision maker, and the [intermediaries](/lessons/4-knowledge-brokers) who turn them into options.** Ask who will use a review and for which decision, and pay for a short version with decision-relevant findings first. Expect a wait for a [policy window](/lessons/6-policy-windows): HHS accepted the needle exchange reviews' findings and kept the funding ban.
- **Which path is this project on?** If it depends on one researcher, what can others use without them? If it is one study among many, who will pull the studies together, and for whom?

## Where it may not hold

The classification is our judgment. We chose only cases in which research helped change policy or practice, so persistent researchers whose tools went unused, and studies that never added up to a change, are missing. The eight were also [chosen for relatively short causal chains](/method#what-we-favored) and well-documented pathways, which may overstate individuals' roles: famous researchers leave better records, and three case pages trace a laureate's role partly through Nobel committee documents.

A tool can also work in ways its author did not first argue. Kremer's 2000 case was for spurring research on vaccines not yet developed, but the pilot backed nearly ready ones. Evaluators found it did little for research but led firms to expand capacity, through supply commitments the expert group recommended. He and two fellow members reply that the best design depends on how far along a product is.

Neither path describes research built with the organization that would use it, as Creating Moves to Opportunity, MTO's follow-up, was with two housing authorities and New Haven's evaluation with the exchange's director. The set was chosen to underweight [such partnerships](/lessons/1-public-engagement), which is where much social science creates value.

## What others have found

Much of this echoes research on policy entrepreneurs, advocates who, in the account of political scientist John Kingdon of the University of Michigan, invest time, energy and reputation in a proposal over years {{cite:kingdon1984}}. The researchers here persisted, but others did much of the carrying, as advice to academics, condensed in one review, puts it: "be 'entrepreneurial' or find someone who is" {{cite:oliver-cairney2019}}.

The path-two decisions mostly show what sociologist Carol Weiss of Harvard called the problem-solving and political models of research use, answering officials' questions or backing positions they held, rather than enlightenment, the slow change in how people understand a problem {{cite:weiss1979}}. Interviews with 29 health care managers and policymakers suggested they would benefit from reviews that highlight decision-relevant information {{cite:lavis2005}}.
