## In the cases

**A Treasury official and a policy project.** Employers had begun enrolling workers in [retirement plans](/case-studies/behavioral-nudges) unless they opted out, and the Internal Revenue Service approved the practice in 1998, before the main studies appeared. J. Mark Iwry, Treasury's benefits tax counsel from 1995 to 2001, directed the department's strategy to approve and promote it, his Brookings biography says. In 2005 Iwry and two Brookings Institution economists drew on the studies for the Retirement Security Project's automatic 401(k) proposal, and in 2006 Congress overrode restrictive state laws, as their brief asked.

**A staff economist inside the regulator.** Evan Kwerel, an economist who joined the [Federal Communications Commission](/case-studies/fcc-spectrum) (FCC) in 1983, proposed auctioning airwave licenses in 1985 with colleague Alex Felker, and led the design group after Congress authorized auctions in 1993. By Stanford economist Paul Milgrom's account, Kwerel favored a format drawn largely from proposals by Milgrom and fellow Stanford economist Robert Wilson and by Preston McAfee, then at the University of Texas at Austin. Major bidders had hired all three, and Chairman Reed Hundt adopted the format with other staff's endorsement.

**A policy study, an advocacy group and the White House.** In 1988 Senators Timothy Wirth and John Heinz created [Project 88](/case-studies/emissions-trading), directed by Harvard Kennedy School economist Robert Stavins, a former Environmental Defense Fund (EDF) official; it proposed capping sulfur dioxide emissions and letting firms trade permits. EDF staff worked with the White House on the first allowance-trading proposal, and economists there championed market tools. Other environmental groups and most Democrats opposed market-based policies, and White House participants later said the program needed EDF's credibility in Congress.

## The pattern

By an intermediary, or knowledge broker, we mean anyone who made findings into something a decision maker could act on and took it to them, whether or not they produced the research. Carriers were of three kinds. Some were researchers carrying their own work, such as economist Michael Kremer, then at Harvard, who co-chaired the Center for Global Development (CGD) working group that drafted the terms of an [advance market commitment](/case-studies/amc-vaccines) (a pledge to buy vaccines that meet set standards). Others were advocacy groups, think tanks and policy projects, such as EDF, CGD and the Retirement Security Project.

A third kind sat inside government, and some, like Kwerel, shared in the decision. Two White House officials, economist Michael Greenstone and legal scholar Cass Sunstein, co-led the working group that set the [social cost of carbon](/case-studies/dice-modeling), the future damage from one more ton of carbon dioxide, discounted to today's dollars. Government staff in the group ran the published Dynamic Integrated Climate-Economy (DICE) model and two others.

Not every case needed a carrier. In [Moving to Opportunity](/case-studies/moving-to-opportunity), members of Congress cited the research directly, and Seattle and King County's housing authorities developed a follow-up program's services with the researchers. In New Haven, the [needle exchange](/case-studies/needle-exchange)'s director coauthored its evaluation, and in Peru, researchers worked with the education ministry on a [growth mindset](/case-studies/growth-mindset) session, though US schools got the work mostly from outside groups, two of them started by mindset researchers. Where the people who would act helped build the research, there was little to carry, a route [lesson 1](/lessons/1-public-engagement) takes up.

## Why it is easy to miss

Credit tends to go to the study, and its carriers are hard to see: political scientist John Kingdon of the University of Michigan placed academics, career civil servants, congressional staff and lower-level appointees in a "hidden cluster" that generates policy alternatives more than it sets agendas {{cite:kingdon2014}}. Accounts of knowledge brokers, such as that of Vivian Tseng, then vice president for program at the William T. Grant Foundation, more often picture outside organizations: advocacy groups, think tanks and consultants that package research for legislators and agency staff {{cite:tseng2012}}.

Carriers also seldom took decision makers a journal article. They took worked-out proposals, such as a cap-and-trade plan, draft contract terms or an automatic 401(k), which decision makers then reshaped, so funders counting papers miss this work.

## What it means for funders and research leaders

- **Pay for the step from findings to a proposal for a named decision.** The Gates Foundation funded CGD's working group, and The Pew Charitable Trusts supported the Retirement Security Project. A review of reviews found no effect from passive dissemination alone {{cite:langer2016}}.
- **Back researchers who take their work to the people who decide.** Kremer made the vaccine case in a policy brief and a book with economist Rachel Glennerster, now president of CGD. Ask applicants which official asked for the work or would act on it, and fund defined engagements tied to that decision.
- **Support routes into government, short and long.** A Council of Economic Advisers seat put MIT economist Richard Schmalensee inside the acid rain debate from 1989 to 1991; a staff post let Kwerel carry the auction idea from 1985 to 1993. Funding researchers' leaves can open short routes; training graduates for agency careers builds long ones.
- **Choose messengers the skeptics trust, and know the limits.** Ask whom the most doubtful decision maker will believe, but do not expect a trusted messenger to settle a fight over values.

## Where it may not hold

We chose only cases in which change happened, and similar carriers may have backed ideas that failed, so the cases cannot show that intermediaries make success more likely. The set leans toward economics, policy and market design, where research [often enters through a rule, an auction, a contract or a model](/method#what-we-favored), which favors carriers inside government. We may also have missed some, such as the benefits consultants our automatic enrollment sources mention only in passing.

The insiders mostly shaped how to act after elected officials or a court decided to act, the same order [lesson 1](/lessons/1-public-engagement) finds in close work with decision makers. Their work could also be undone when administrations changed, as [lesson 5](/lessons/5-legitimacy-narrative) shows for the social cost of carbon.

Carriers have interests. In growth mindset, researchers founded Mindset Works, which sold its program to schools. One meta-analysis (a study pooling many studies' results) reports that authors with a known financial incentive (ties to program sellers or promoters, paid speaking or book profits) more often found positive effects, a coding another team disputes. Most academic economists in the FCC case were bidders' consultants; [lesson 1](/lessons/1-public-engagement) weighs whether that bent their advice.

Credible carriers were not always enough. After four reviews for Congress and federal agencies, the Department of Health and Human Services accepted the needle-exchange evidence in April 1998, yet the administration kept the federal funding restriction. For carbon, EDF joined business leaders in drafting a national cap-and-trade plan {{cite:skocpol2013}}, but in July 2010 the Senate abandoned a House-passed cap-and-trade bill. Political scientist Theda Skocpol of Harvard blames the Senate's 60-vote bar and regional divisions among Democrats, and faults the coalition's insider bargaining {{cite:skocpol2013}}.

## What others have found

Other research points the same way. Tseng notes that agencies and legislators often turn to trusted peers and intermediaries {{cite:tseng2012}}. Reviewing earlier work, sociologists Daniel Hirschman and Elizabeth Popp Berman, then of the University of Michigan and the University at Albany, trace much of economics' policy influence to indirect channels, such as "the institutional position of economists in government" {{cite:hirschman-berman2014}}.

Whether paying brokers works is less clear. A systematic review judged the evidence on brokers' effectiveness in health settings inconclusive {{cite:bornbaum2015}}. In a trial with 108 Canadian public health departments, only tailored messages, not a broker, measurably changed programs, though brokering trended positive where departments placed little value on research {{cite:dobbins2009}}.
