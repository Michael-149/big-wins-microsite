## In the cases

**What spread was not what was tested.** In a 2016 online poll of more than 600 teachers, 77% said they were familiar or very familiar with [growth mindset](/case-studies/growth-mindset), and the practice they most often reported was praising students for taking risks and persevering. That was 3 years before a national study reported that two short online sessions slightly raised lower-achieving ninth graders' grades. An independent trial of a teacher-delivered version in 101 English primary schools found no added progress in literacy or numeracy.

**A message against the evidence.** In 1998, opponents of [needle exchanges](/case-studies/needle-exchange) cited Canadian studies finding more HIV among exchange users; two of the authors replied that officials had misread their research. Congress lifted the federal funding ban in December 2009 and restored it in December 2011. We found no reason stated by Congress; critics said such programs sent the wrong message.

**A federal number with no statute behind it.** From 2010 to 2025, US agencies used versions of a [social cost of carbon](/case-studies/dice-modeling) (the future damage from one more ton of carbon dioxide, discounted to today's dollars) in rule analyses. No statute set it: executive orders withdrew it in 2017, restored its working group in 2021 and withdrew it again in 2025, the last citing, among other reasons, no basis in legislation.

**Permanent law that held.** In [emissions trading](/case-studies/emissions-trading), Title IV of the 1990 Clean Air Act Amendments set a permanent cap on power plant sulfur dioxide, and as of November 2026 it remains law, though demand for its allowances (tradable permits to emit) has all but vanished. The 2006 Pension Protection Act wrote terms for [automatic enrollment](/case-studies/behavioral-nudges) (enrolling workers in a retirement plan unless they opt out) into US law, and Britain's Pensions Act 2008 made it a duty on employers.

## The pattern

By legitimacy we mean whether those who must fund, use or defend a change accept it, which differs from whether its evidence is strong. The cases show two things alongside it: the version of an idea that circulated, and the legal form a change took.

The problem was drift from the tested version rather than running ahead of a test. Drug users, activists and clinicians started needle exchanges before researchers measured them. In [Moving to Opportunity](/case-studies/moving-to-opportunity), studies of Chicago's Gautreaux program, a court-ordered housing remedy, suggested that families who moved to the suburbs did better, though the gains might reflect who moved; a lottery-based federal test then found no change in adults' earnings but, about 20 years later, higher earnings for children who moved young.

In growth mindset, what teachers did under the name was not what the national study tested. Over a third of comparison-school teachers in the English trial had attended growth mindset training days, one of the evaluators' explanations for finding no gain. Only here is drift clear, so the lesson's first half rests mainly on this case.

On durability, Title IV and the pension laws held, while three changes resting on executive orders, a yearly spending law or time-limited authority were reversed or interrupted: the carbon value, twice; the 2009 lifting of the exchange ban; and the Federal Communications Commission's general auction authority for [spectrum licenses](/case-studies/fcc-spectrum), which lapsed from 2023 until a 2025 budget law restored it. Legal form also lined up with political agreement, so these cases cannot separate the two.

The 1990 amendments passed 89 to 10 in the Senate and 401 to 25 in the House, and Title IV's free allowances helped build support, while the carbon value changed with each administration. Yearly spending laws, which this lesson counts as weaker footing, also held for long stretches: they restricted federal money for exchanges from 1989 to 2009, and the ban's partial lifting in December 2015 was kept for fiscal 2026.

## Why it is easy to miss

When 30 US cities ran 73 randomized trials (which assign people to groups by chance) with a national nudge unit (a team applying behavioral science for governments), the strength of a trial's evidence did not strongly predict adoption, though researchers and practitioners surveyed beforehand expected it to matter more {{cite:dellavigna2024,dellavigna2022wp}}. Journals can also run ahead of practice: in 126 trials by two US nudge units, mostly letters, reminders and simpler forms, with the one default trial set aside, the average nudge raised take-up (the share who acted) by 1.4 percentage points, against 8.7 in academic journals, mostly because of selective publication; the authors still judge it sizable {{cite:dellavigna2022}}.

Political scientist Sharece Thrower notes that scholars have largely overlooked how easily later administrations can change executive orders {{cite:thrower2017}}. Outside review and a court ruling were no substitute for a statute: the National Academies reviewed the carbon method and an appeals court upheld its use in 2016, yet it was withdrawn twice.

## What it means for funders and research leaders

- **Test the version that is spreading.** Pay to learn what practitioners do under an idea's name, and test that version independently, as the English trial did.
- **Count every trial.** Require analysis plans registered before results are known, and the release of null results.
- **Give a change more than one anchor.** Ask early what legal footing a change will rest on, and add others: Washington and Colorado wrote carbon values into state law, and exchanges spread on state, local and private money while federal funds were restricted.
- **Plan for misreading.** Release a plain note on what a high-stakes study does not show, and answer misreadings in public, as two of the Canadian authors did in 1998.
- **Ask of your own projects.** If the next administration or Congress wanted to undo this change, who would defend it?

## Where it may not hold

These cases show stories and legal forms alongside outcomes, not their causal effect: no case measured a framing effect (a change in opinion caused by how an issue is presented), and stories may have followed decisions rather than shaped them. Not every reversal shows a story beating the evidence: Scott County, Indiana, commissioners voted in 2021 to end their exchange, saying it led to more overdoses, an effect one national study reported and others dispute.

Law is not the only anchor. The [advance market commitment](/case-studies/amc-vaccines) for pneumococcal vaccines, a donor pledge to top up prices in return for firms' promises of up to 10 years of supply, rested on contracts, not a statute, and ran until it closed at the end of 2020. Nor is law always enough: executive action narrowed what federal health grants may buy in 2025 and 2026, while Congress kept the December 2015 exchange provision.

Because these eight were chosen from changes that happened, ideas with a good story that never spread are absent, and eight cases cannot say how often the pattern holds. The legal forms are mostly American, and the set underrepresents slow shifts in how a field or the public understands a problem, where narrative may matter most.

## What others have found

Research on framing {{cite:chong2007}} and policy narratives {{cite:jones2010}} studies how the way an issue is told shifts opinion, and the political model of research use described by sociologist Carol Weiss of Harvard, in which findings become ammunition in a debate, fits the misread Canadian studies {{cite:weiss1979}}. What spread under growth mindset's name may have kept the label but lost the core, the parts that implementation scientists separate from a periphery that can adapt to a setting {{cite:damschroder2009}} (see [lesson 3](/lessons/3-design-details)).

The durability literature qualifies the lesson. Thrower found that an executive order's survival depends on its features and on later shifts in the political climate {{cite:thrower2017}}, and a study of federal programs found restructurings and terminations commonplace, especially when the partisan makeup of Congress changes {{cite:berry2010}}. Political scientists Eric Patashnik and Paul Pierson argue that what lasts depends on how a policy reshapes the interests and incentives around it {{cite:patashnik2008,pierson1993}}, as Title IV's free allowances suggest.
