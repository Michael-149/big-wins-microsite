## The problem

Pneumococcus, a bacterium, is a leading cause of bacterial pneumonia, meningitis and sepsis in young children {{cite:obrien2009}}. A 2009 study estimated that in 2000 it killed about 826,000 children aged 1 to 59 months (range 582,000 to 926,000) {{cite:obrien2009}}.

In 2000 the United States licensed a pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV), a new type that, unlike older pneumococcal vaccines, works in infants {{cite:cgd2005}}. In 2009 it sold for over $70 a dose in rich countries {{cite:gavi-2009-launch}}. Gavi, the Vaccine Alliance, says such complex vaccines would normally reach lower-income countries 10 to 15 years after high-income ones {{cite:gavi-amc-page}}.

Launched in 2000 to help poor countries get vaccines {{cite:kff-gavi-2026}}, Gavi was already working on such delays: from 2003 its Pneumococcal Accelerated Development and Introduction Plan (PneumoADIP), at Johns Hopkins University, worked to speed access to these vaccines, and one of its researchers led the Gavi-funded 2009 death estimate {{cite:gavi-adip-eval,obrien2009}}. Large trials in South Africa and The Gambia showed conjugate vaccines protected African children {{cite:klugman2003,cutts2005}}; in The Gambia, vaccinated children were an estimated 16% less likely to die than those given a placebo {{cite:cutts2005}}.

## The research

In 2000, Michael Kremer, then a Harvard economist and now at the University of Chicago {{cite:web-kremer}}, published two working papers on vaccines for malaria, tuberculosis and HIV {{cite:kremer2000a,kremer2000b}}. Promising in advance to buy such vaccines was not a new idea: the World Health Organization (WHO) had discussed it in 1996, a coalition coordinated by the International AIDS Vaccine Initiative had urged it at the 1997 Group of Eight summit, and in 2000 the World Bank's president floated a $1 billion purchase fund {{cite:kremer2000a}}. Kremer set out the economic case: little research went into such vaccines partly because once firms had sunk money into research, governments as regulators and major buyers would be tempted to push prices below research costs {{cite:kremer2000a}}.

His proposed commitment to buy a vaccine meeting set standards, with co-payments from countries, meant the public would pay only if a successful vaccine was developed {{cite:kremer2000a,kremer2000b}}. The National Science Foundation and the MacArthur Foundation funded the work {{cite:kremer2000a}}.

He and economist Rachel Glennerster made the case in a 2000 Brookings policy brief and a 2004 book, Strong Medicine {{cite:glennerster-kremer2000,kremer-glennerster2004}}. From 2003 to 2005, the Center for Global Development (CGD), a think tank Glennerster was named to lead in 2024, convened a working group funded by the Gates Foundation to turn the idea into a proposal {{cite:cgd2005,cgd-amc-page,cgd-glennerster-2024,web-glennerster}}.

Health economist Ruth Levine of CGD, Kremer and Alice Albright, chief financial officer of the Vaccine Fund, Gavi's funding arm, chaired it {{cite:cgd2005}}. Its 2005 report, Making Markets for Vaccines, set out an advance market commitment (AMC) for early-stage vaccines and late-stage ones such as pneumococcal vaccine, with draft contract terms {{cite:cgd2005}}.

## From research to change

Finance ministries gave the idea political backing, while donors and the experts who developed it kept it moving, Gavi's 2013 design evaluation found {{cite:dalberg2013}}. In 2005 it caught the attention of Gordon Brown, then the UK's Chancellor of the Exchequer, and Italy took the lead, supplying nearly half the money {{cite:dalberg2013}}. That December, Group of Seven (G7) finance ministers welcomed a report on advance market commitments by Italy's finance minister, Giulio Tremonti, and agreed to develop a pilot {{cite:g7-2005}}.

In February 2006, an independent expert committee recommended pneumococcal disease as the pilot's target, and that year Gavi decided to offer the vaccine, in effect already committing to support uptake {{cite:gavi-2007-launch,gavi-amc-timeline,dalberg2021}}. Five governments and the Gates Foundation, led by Italy and the UK, committed $1.5 billion in February 2007 {{cite:gavi-2007-launch}}.

In May 2007 the donors formed a 10-member Economic Expert Group, chaired by David Fleming, a public health official from Seattle and King County; its economists included Kremer, Christopher Snyder of Dartmouth College and Jonathan Levin, then a Stanford associate professor and, since 2024, Stanford's president {{cite:eeg2008,eeg-donor-2008,web-snyder,web-levin}}. The group found the November 2006 draft design "unlikely to lead to significant build-out of manufacturing capacity" and recommended tying the money to firms' long-term supply commitments {{cite:eeg2008}}. Kremer, Levin and Snyder later called pneumococcus a "technologically close target", with vaccines for developing-country strains in late-stage trials, so the main challenge, they argue, was factory capacity, not research {{cite:kremer2020-wp}}.

An Implementation Working Group, joining four of the expert group's members with staff of Gavi, the United Nations Children's Fund and the World Bank, then set the price with the donors {{cite:iwg2008,dalberg2013}}. Each firm promised to supply a set number of doses a year for up to 10 years at a price, called the tail price, of no more than $3.50 a dose; in return, the fund paid an extra $3.50 a dose on the first 21% of each contract's doses {{cite:dalberg2013,iwg2008}}. Gavi paid the tail price, with small country co-payments; during design the ceiling rose far above early estimates and co-payments were cut by about 80%, leaving Gavi to pay about $3.30 a dose in the first contracts {{cite:dalberg2013}}.

## The impact

GlaxoSmithKline (GSK) and Pfizer signed the first supply agreements in March 2010, and that December Nicaragua became the first country to introduce the vaccine under the AMC {{cite:gavi-supply-agreements,gavi-amc-2020}}. Supply rose from 3 million doses a year in 2010 to 150 million in 2017, as the firms invested about $500 million in capacity, partly planned before the AMC {{cite:dalberg2021}}. Gavi's 2015 evaluation, by the Boston Consulting Group, found the firms expanded capacity in response to the AMC and its supply agreements {{cite:bcg2015-summary}}.

GSK's and Pfizer's newer vaccines became available in Gavi countries a year after rich countries {{cite:bcg2015-summary}}; the first pneumococcal conjugate vaccine had reached them only in 2009, donated to The Gambia and Rwanda {{cite:gavi-amc-2020}}. Coverage in that period was three to four times that of vaccines against rotavirus, a diarrheal virus, and Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib), another bacterium that kills young children {{cite:bcg2015-summary,wahl2018}}.

By the end of 2019, Gavi estimates, more than 215 million children had been immunized with AMC-supported vaccine {{cite:gavi-amc-2020}}. When the AMC closed on December 31, 2020, it had committed $1.31 billion in subsidies to three manufacturers, and 60 of the 73 eligible lower-income countries had introduced the vaccine {{cite:gavi-amc-2020,dalberg2021}}. India, which began a phased rollout only in 2017, completed national introduction in November 2021 {{cite:gavi-india-2021}}. Dalberg, the consultancy behind Gavi's final evaluation, credits the vaccine, not the AMC alone, with about 752,000 lives saved in its first 11 years in the 73 countries, using one of two models; the other is more conservative, and neither counts protection of unvaccinated people {{cite:dalberg2021}}.

Prices fell in steps: GSK's to $3.05 by 2017 and Pfizer's multi-dose vials to $2.90 by 2019; Gavi projects these cuts below the $3.50 ceiling will save $546 million over agreements that run to 2029 {{cite:gavi-amc-2020,dalberg2021}}. In 2020 the Serum Institute of India joined at $2.00 a dose for its five-dose vial, with a vaccine developed over more than a decade with the health organization PATH and Gates Foundation funding {{cite:gavi-2020-sii,gavi-amc-2020,path-2019}}.

Gavi's 2020 COVAX commitment for COVID-19 vaccines built on the design, though Dalberg says COVAX "is different in material ways" {{cite:gavi-covax-amc,dalberg2021}}. In 2024 Gavi launched the African Vaccine Manufacturing Accelerator, which pays African vaccine makers a per-dose top-up; the board presentation proposing it cited the pneumococcal AMC's governance as an example {{cite:gavi-avma,gavi-avma-board-2023}}.

## What is still debated

**Effect on new vaccines.** Speeding the development of vaccines suited to developing countries was the pilot's first formal objective, and Médecins Sans Frontières (MSF) argued in 2020 that the AMC "was flawed from the outset" because it chose a disease that already had a vaccine {{cite:dalberg2021,msf2020}}. On research and development, the 2015 evaluation found "very little influence", noting that two qualifying vaccines were already in advanced development, and Dalberg found no evidence of a faster pipeline {{cite:bcg2015-summary,dalberg2021}}. Donald Light, a professor of comparative health care systems at the University of Medicine and Dentistry of New Jersey who sat on the CGD group but withheld his endorsement, had warned in 2005 that a commitment suits late-stage products and is a slower spur to discovery than direct research funding {{cite:light2005}}.

Kremer, Levin and Snyder reply that political factors may favor close targets, and that the best design depends on how far along a product is {{cite:kremer2020-wp,kremer2022}}. The 2015 and 2021 evaluations also found other effects: the AMC drove new multi-dose vials suited to poor countries and showed firms that a large market would outlast it {{cite:dalberg2021,bcg2015-summary}}.

**How much firms were paid.** MSF, which campaigned from 2015 for a lower price, says $1.238 billion, 82% of the fund, went to Pfizer and GSK, whose prices fell only about 15% over a decade, and that shortages of up to 29 million doses in 2012 to 2014 delayed 23 countries' introductions {{cite:msf-fairshot2016,msf2020}}. MSF blames firms for building only to contracted volumes, which rested on low early forecasts; the 2015 evaluators found shortages were fixed faster than without the AMC {{cite:msf2020,bcg2015-summary}}. Dalberg found weak price competition, likely because the Serum Institute's vaccine arrived 5 years later than expected and subsidies were linked to volume, not price; the pilot, it notes, sought "low, long term and sustainable prices" rather than competition {{cite:dalberg2021}}.

The three economists answer that the right benchmark is the least a firm would accept, not its manufacturing cost, and that offering less "risks children not receiving lifesaving vaccines" {{cite:kremer2020-wp}}. By their calibration, $3.50 beats $2 even if the lower price carried only a 4% risk of firms refusing, though the figure depends on how savings would be used {{cite:kremer2020-wp}}. They concede that "at least one firm would likely have participated at a lower price", and that lower co-payments, wider eligibility and faster tenders "could potentially have generated greater health benefits" {{cite:kremer2020-wp}}. The 2013 evaluators judged the ceiling likely right for getting both firms to take part, while their simulations put firms' returns at or above a 10 to 20% target in most scenarios {{cite:dalberg2013}}.

**How much was due to the AMC.** Kremer, Levin and Snyder write that the vaccine's cost-effectiveness "does not prove the cost effectiveness of the overall AMC because we lack a valid counterfactual", though they estimate that at rotavirus vaccine's slower pace, 67 million fewer infants would have been immunized {{cite:kremer2020-wp}}. The 2015 evaluators also credit PneumoADIP, Gavi's Accelerated Vaccine Introduction initiative and WHO's strong prior recommendation, and say results cannot be attributed "exclusively" to the AMC {{cite:bcg2015-summary}}. Few country decision makers knew of the AMC or cited its supply and price promises; Dalberg concludes it "worked, but not through the causal path assumed by Gavi" {{cite:dalberg2021}}. Gavi commissioned all three evaluations {{cite:dalberg2013,gavi-amc-2020,dalberg2021}}.

**Who was left out.** MSF faults the AMC for having no plan to transfer vaccine technology to developing-country makers, and calls for a strategy for countries outside Gavi support {{cite:msf2020}}. Kremer and his coauthors acknowledge that extending the AMC beyond Gavi-eligible countries "could have saved more lives", though firms might have demanded higher prices {{cite:kremer2020-wp}}.

**Who pays now.** The 2013 evaluators found Gavi's payments for the vaccine "strained GAVI's business model severely and were a leading cause of its 2011 funding crisis" {{cite:dalberg2013}}. The vaccine was expected to take about 28% of Gavi's program spending in 2016 to 2020, and the 2021 evaluation questioned whether countries can sustain their share {{cite:gavi-amc-2020,dalberg2021}}. The United States withheld $600 million from Gavi, citing vaccine safety concerns, then said in late July 2026 that it would release it {{cite:kff-gavi-2026,gavi-us-2026}}.

**Where our account may be incomplete.** We relied on published sources. Firms' production costs are confidential, so the returns above are simulations, not company accounts {{cite:dalberg2013}}. The 2013 evaluators also found that the analyses behind the price had not then been made public, so the payment structure "is still the subject of controversy" {{cite:dalberg2013}}.

## What this case teaches

[Design details](/lessons/3-design-details) shaped results: finding the draft weak on capacity, the expert group recommended tying subsidy money to firms' promises of up to 10 years of supply, and firms expanded capacity in response {{cite:eeg2008,bcg2015-summary}}. Paying by volume rather than price, evaluators say, helped keep price competition weak, along with a third supplier's late arrival {{cite:dalberg2021}}.

Kremer fits the first of [two paths](/lessons/2-archetypes), a persistent researcher. He gave an existing idea its economic case and carried it from papers and a book to the donors' expert group.

[Intermediaries](/lessons/4-knowledge-brokers) carried it to decision makers. A foundation-funded think tank turned papers into a blueprint, and finance ministers took it to the G7 {{cite:dalberg2013,g7-2005}}.

Research did [a different job at each stage](/lessons/8-different-roles), from the economic case to the evaluations, and expert-group members helped set the price. The idea and vaccines came from elsewhere: two vaccines were nearly ready, and the third came from years of development funding.
