summary: "Needle exchanges began outside research, with a drug users' group in Amsterdam in 1984, followed by a Sydney physician, Britain's government and US activists. Studies in New Haven, Connecticut, in New York City, in Britain and across 99 cities linked them to lower HIV risk, and reviews found no evidence that they increased drug use. A US federal funding ban from 1988 was lifted in 2009, restored in 2011 and partly lifted in December 2015, after an HIV outbreak in Indiana; federal support grew after that and has narrowed since 2025."

## The problem

Before AIDS was known, HIV spread among people who injected drugs in New York City at an estimated 13 new infections a year per 100 people, and by the mid-1980s about half had HIV. Buying a syringe there required a prescription {{cite:desjarlais2016}}. By April 1998, nearly 40% of the 652,000 AIDS cases reported in the United States had been linked to injection drug use {{cite:hhs-1998}}.

Needle exchanges, now often called syringe services programs, are a form of harm reduction (help that lowers the dangers of drug use without requiring people to stop). They give people who inject drugs sterile syringes, traditionally in return for used ones, and many offer testing and treatment referrals {{cite:kff-ssp-2025,hhs-1998,ruiz2019}}.

The idea came from outside research. In 1984 the Junkie Union, a drug users' group in Amsterdam, started the first exchange to prevent hepatitis B {{cite:lurie1993}}. In Sydney, physician Alex Wodak of St Vincent's Hospital opened an illegal exchange in 1986 {{cite:adf-2019,web-wodak}}. After an HIV outbreak among Edinburgh injectors {{cite:robertson1986,stimson1995}}, Britain's government began trial syringe schemes in 1987 {{cite:stimson1988}}, and in 1988 its Advisory Council on the Misuse of Drugs judged HIV a greater danger to health than drug misuse {{cite:kalk2017}}.

In the United States, Yale public health student Jon Parker began handing out needles publicly in 1986, and Dave Purchase, who later founded the North American Syringe Exchange Network, started an exchange in Tacoma, Washington, in 1988 {{cite:lurie1993,yale-library-exhibit,heraldnet-2013}}. That year Congress barred funds under a new AIDS law from supplying needles for illegal drug use unless the Surgeon General found an exchange would reduce both drug abuse and HIV risk, and spending laws restricted federal money for exchanges from 1989 {{cite:law-1988,hhs-1998}}.

## The research

In 1990 physicians, public health experts, policymakers and advocates persuaded Connecticut's legislature to try a legal, city-run exchange in New Haven, which opened that November {{cite:kaplanheimer1992,kaplan1993,web-heimer,yale-library-exhibit}}. Because researchers judged it unethical to test clients, Edward Kaplan, an operations researcher (a builder of mathematical models for decisions) at Yale's management school, proposed testing the needles, which also kept clients anonymous {{cite:yale-library-exhibit,web-kaplan}}. Robert Heimer, a Yale pharmacologist and epidemiologist, tested coded, returned syringes for HIV genetic material {{cite:heimer1993,yale-library-exhibit}}. The share carrying it fell from more than two-thirds to under 45% within 3 months, a drop the authors found was not explained by a shift in clients {{cite:heimer1993,kaplan1994ajph}}.

Kaplan's models estimated that the exchange cut the HIV infection rate among clients by at least a third, a lower bound rather than a count {{cite:kaplan1993,kaplan1994}}. It was the first federally funded evaluation of a US exchange, and the program's director, Elaine O'Keefe, coauthored the main paper {{cite:heimer1993,kaplan1993,yale-library-exhibit}}.

Reviewing 1987 to 1993, sociologist Gerry Stimson of Charing Cross and Westminster Medical School, London, reported HIV in about 20% of British injectors in Edinburgh (down from 55%), 7% in London and 1% elsewhere {{cite:stimson1995}}. He credits syringe schemes, methadone and outreach, while noting that the link is hard to prove {{cite:stimson1995}}. Across 99 cities, HIV prevalence among injectors fell 18.6% a year where exchanges opened and rose 8.1% where they never did, a whole-city comparison whose limits its authors acknowledge {{cite:macdonald2003}}.

## From research to change

According to the New Haven authors, Connecticut's legislature kept funding the program, extended exchanges to Bridgeport and Hartford and decriminalized syringe possession, and programs and laws in New York City, California and Massachusetts followed "partially as a result" of their findings {{cite:kaplan1993}}. In New York City, activists opened small exchanges, and in 1992 the state health department began licensing and funding exchanges with the city's agreement {{cite:desjarlais2016}}.

Reviews for Congress and federal agencies followed. In 1993 the General Accounting Office found Kaplan's model "technically sound" and its estimate "defensible," concluding only that exchanges "may hold some promise" {{cite:gao1993}}. A University of California review for the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), co-led by physician Peter Lurie of the San Francisco campus, judged that the model probably underestimated the effect, found no evidence that exchanges increased drug use and urged repeal of the funding ban {{cite:lurie1993}}. A 1995 National Research Council and Institute of Medicine panel requested by Congress, and a 1997 panel convened by the National Institutes of Health, also urged lifting it {{cite:nrc1995,nih1997}}.

In April 1998, Donna Shalala, Secretary of Health and Human Services (HHS), determined that exchanges can help reduce HIV transmission and "do not encourage the use of illegal drugs." Yet the administration kept the restriction, telling communities to "use their own dollars" {{cite:hhs-1998}}.

Exchanges kept spreading with state, local and private money, to 28 states and one territory by 1998 {{cite:hhs-1998}}. Congress lifted the ban in December 2009, leaving local officials a veto over sites, and restored it in December 2011 {{cite:law-fy2010,law-fy2012}}. We found no reason stated by Congress; critics said such programs "send the wrong message" {{cite:kffhn-2011}}.

Scott County, Indiana, had reported fewer than five HIV cases a year {{cite:conrad2015}}. From November 2014 to November 2015, 181 people were diagnosed in an outbreak, most of whom had injected the prescription opioid oxymorphone {{cite:peters2016}}. Exchanges were illegal in Indiana, and Gov. Mike Pence had opposed them as a drug strategy. But on the advice of CDC and the state health department, led by Commissioner Jerome Adams, he declared an emergency on March 26, 2015, and the county's exchange opened on April 4 {{cite:isdh-2015-mar27,wfyi-2015-mar,isdh-2015-apr4}}.

That December, Congress let federal funds pay for exchange costs other than needles and syringes where health departments, consulting CDC, find a risk of hepatitis or HIV outbreaks {{cite:law-fy2016}}. Its explanatory statement gave no reason {{cite:crec-2015}}, but researchers say it was made "largely in response to HIV outbreaks among suburban and rural populations injecting prescription opioids" {{cite:ruiz2019}}. In 2016 CDC scientists ranked every US county and named 220 in 26 states most vulnerable to rapid HIV or hepatitis C spread among people who inject drugs {{cite:vanhandel2016}}.

## The impact

In 2013, 204 programs were known {{cite:desjarlais2015}}. In mid-2025 the directory of the network Purchase founded listed 522 in 43 states, Washington, DC, and Puerto Rico, a self-reported, likely incomplete count {{cite:kff-ssp-2025}}.

In New York City from 1990 to 2002, syringes exchanged each year rose from 250,000 to 3 million while estimated new HIV infections among injectors fell from 3.55 to 0.77 per 100 person-years (one person followed for a year), in data from Beth Israel Medical Center epidemiologist Don Des Jarlais and colleagues {{cite:desjarlais2005}}. They credit "combined prevention" (exchanges, methadone or buprenorphine treatment, and HIV medicines) with sharply cutting new infections there and in Vancouver and France {{cite:desjarlais2016}}.

In Scott County, sharing of injection equipment fell after a response that combined the exchange with testing, contact tracing and treatment {{cite:dasgupta2019,gonsalves2018}}. A modeling study estimated that starting the response in January 2013 could have averted at least 127 of an estimated 183 to 184 infections by August 2015 {{cite:gonsalves2018}}.

A model estimated that Australia's programs averted 19,000 to 77,000 hepatitis C and 192 to 873 HIV infections from 2000 to 2010, assuming far more syringe sharing without them {{cite:kwon2012}}. The A$70 to A$220 million in health care costs they saved that decade (2010 Australian dollars) was less than their A$245 million cost; the authors project payback by 2032 {{cite:kwon2012}}. After Philadelphia legalized its exchange in 1992, HIV diagnoses among people who inject drugs ran 10,592 below a forecast through 2002, a gap one study valued at $2.4 billion or more in lifetime treatment costs (2012 dollars) {{cite:ruiz2019,schackman2015}}.

Federal backing grew, then reversed. CDC scientists called exchanges "an essential component" of ending the HIV epidemic {{cite:broz2021}}, and the 2022 national drug strategy said its harm reduction focus "addresses a historic gap in past U.S. drug policy" {{cite:ndcs-2022}}. In July 2025 an executive order told HHS that discretionary grants from the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration (SAMHSA) must not fund harm reduction efforts that "only facilitate illegal drug use and its attendant harm" {{cite:eo-14321}}; in April 2026 SAMHSA said no HHS funding may buy or distribute syringes for illicit drug use, drug test strips or safer smoking supplies {{cite:samhsa-2026}}. Congress kept the 2015 provision for fiscal 2026, and a stopgap law carries it to December 11, 2026 {{cite:law-fy2026,law-cr-fy2027}}.

In 2021 Scott County's commissioners voted 2 to 1 to end the exchange from January 2022, saying it led to more overdoses; the county health administrator warned of another outbreak without it {{cite:ipm-2021}}. Indiana renewed its exchange law for 5 years in March 2026 with limits such as proof of county residence and one-for-one exchange; Gov. Mike Braun let it become law unsigned, saying, "I think that's treating a symptom" {{cite:ipm-2026}}.

## What is still debated

The HIV evidence is observational: studies compare people or places rather than assigning exchanges by lottery {{cite:aspinall2014}}. The 1993 University of California review judged that such studies, partly because of obstacles to randomization, "probably cannot provide clear evidence" of an effect {{cite:lurie1993}}.

Wodak and a colleague's 2006 review found "compelling evidence of effectiveness, safety, and cost-effectiveness" {{cite:wodak2006}}. A 2010 review of reviews found only "tentative evidence" on HIV, concluding, "The evidence is weaker than given credit for in the literature" {{cite:palmateer2010}}; the same lead author's 2022 review of studies from 2011 to 2020 rated the HIV evidence "sufficient" {{cite:palmateer2022}}. A 2014 meta-analysis (pooling results across studies) by an overlapping team linked exchange use to about a third less HIV transmission across 12 studies, short of statistical significance, and 58% less in six higher-quality ones {{cite:aspinall2014}}.

For hepatitis C, a 2017 Cochrane review (a standardized systematic review) found the clearest benefit for methadone or buprenorphine treatment, which halved the risk, and 74% lower risk combined with high syringe coverage; exchanges alone helped only in European studies, on low or very low quality evidence {{cite:platt2017}}. A 2023 analysis of cohorts in Amsterdam, Vancouver and Melbourne, designed to mimic trials, found 41% lower HIV risk (Amsterdam and Vancouver only) and 76% lower hepatitis C risk with full use of both {{cite:vansanten2023}}.

In 1998 opponents, including national drug policy chief Barry McCaffrey, cited Canadian studies finding more HIV among exchange users in Montreal and Vancouver {{cite:crec-1998,bruneau1997,strathdee1997}}. Two of the authors replied that officials "have misinterpreted our research," since inner-city exchanges serve users "at greatest risk of infection" {{cite:crec-1998}}, and a 1999 follow-up found no evidence that Vancouver's exchange spread HIV {{cite:schechter1999}}. Still, the Vancouver paper was titled "Needle exchange is not enough," and a coauthor later wrote that this misreading "was used as part of a successful campaign to oppose U.S. federal funding of needle exchange" {{cite:strathdee1997,schechter2002}}.

Opponents also argued that exchanges increase drug use and crime {{cite:crec-1998}}. The 1995 panel found no credible evidence of increased drug use among participants, though that evidence rested mainly on self-reports {{cite:nrc1995}}, and in Baltimore, arrest trends did not differ between areas with and without an exchange {{cite:marx2000}}.

On overdose, economist Analisa Packham of Vanderbilt University found that US exchange openings cut HIV diagnoses by up to 18.2% but raised opioid deaths, most in rural counties and after fentanyl spread {{cite:packham2022}}. Researchers including two from the North American Syringe Exchange Network replied that her methods were too fragile to show cause {{cite:lambdin2023,pollack2023}}; public health researcher Harold Pollack of the University of Chicago agreed, but called her work a good-faith use of standard tools {{cite:pollack2023}}.

All the savings figures are modeled; the Australian team's 2009 government-commissioned report estimated far more infections averted than its 2012 model {{cite:roi2-2009,kwon2012}}. The Philadelphia forecast rests on nine years of earlier data, with no comparison series for the city, and the study credits the whole gap to the exchange {{cite:ruiz2019}}. Our account also has gaps: we found no source showing that Pence cited exchange research, or confirming when Scott County's exchange closed.

## What this case teaches

Research did [a different job at each stage](/lessons/8-different-roles). Drug users, activists, clinicians and governments started exchanges; researchers measured them, informed reviews, answered misreadings and, after 2015, mapped outbreak risk.

Evidence was not enough without a [policy window](/lessons/6-policy-windows). HHS accepted the findings in 1998 and kept the ban. After the 2015 outbreak, a governor who had opposed exchanges allowed one, and researchers credit such outbreaks for that December's federal change.

Change took [decades](/lessons/7-long-timeline). The ban was first lifted 21 years after the 1988 law, for 2 years, and the December 2015 partial lift came 27 years after it. Lurie and a colleague estimated from Australia's experience that a national program might have prevented 4,394 to 9,666 HIV infections from 1987 to 1995, assuming exchanges cut new infections by 15 to 33% {{cite:luriedrucker1997}}.

New Haven's researchers [worked closely with decision makers](/lessons/1-public-engagement). The evaluation was built into the city-run program, whose director coauthored it.
