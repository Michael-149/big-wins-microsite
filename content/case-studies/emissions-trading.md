## The problem

In the 1980s, coal-fired power plants were the main source of sulfur dioxide (SO2), the gas behind the acid rain damaging lakes, forests and buildings in the northeastern United States and southeastern Canada {{cite:ss2012-nber,chan2012}}. Few knew that the same pollution, as fine sulfate particles, was also shortening lives; the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) estimated in 2010 that lower fine-particle levels were saving about 20,000 to 50,000 lives a year {{cite:ss2012-nber,epa-2010-release}}.

Industrial and regional interests blocked more than 70 bills to control SO2 in the 1980s {{cite:burtrawpalmer2003,voss2007-wp}}, and the Senate Majority Leader in 1988, Robert Byrd (D-West Virginia), represented a high-sulfur coal state {{cite:chan2012}}. The National Acid Precipitation Assessment Program, federal research costing more than $500 million, informed the debate but did not directly produce the eventual compromise {{cite:burtrawpalmer2003}}.

## The research

Building on economist Ronald Coase's 1960 analysis of property rights and spillover harms, written at the University of Virginia, US economist Thomas Crocker (1966) and Canadian economist John Dales (1968) proposed a market in rights to emit pollution {{cite:coase1960,web-nobel-coase,chan2012}}. In 1972, economist W. David Montgomery proved that a cap on total emissions with tradable permits meets the target at the lowest total cost, whoever receives the permits first {{cite:montgomery1972,ss2012-nber}}. Firms that can cut cheaply cut more and sell spare permits, later called allowances, to firms for which cutting is expensive {{cite:ss2012-nber}}.

From 1976, EPA let firms offset new pollution with cuts elsewhere, and from 1979 it allowed bubbles (one limit across several sources) and banking credits for later use {{cite:hahnhester1989}}. President Jimmy Carter's EPA chief, Douglas Costle, and official William Drayton backed the experiments, and young EPA economists linked them to the theory, writes governance scholar Jan-Peter Voss of Berlin's Öko-Institut {{cite:voss2007,voss2007-wp}}.

In a 1985 book, economist Thomas Tietenberg of Colby College found EPA's credit trading sporadic and reported simulations where prescriptive, source-by-source rules sometimes cost more than twice the least-cost approach, though these assumed no controls were yet installed {{cite:hahnhester1989,voss2007-wp,chan2012}}. A 1989 review coauthored by Robert Hahn, then a senior staff economist at the White House Council of Economic Advisers, found savings of over a billion dollars, mostly from trades inside single firms {{cite:hahnhester1989}}. It called the failure of active credit markets "the greatest disappointment of emissions trading", blaming uncertain credits and the cost of finding partners and winning approval {{cite:hahnhester1989}}.

Opponents charged that inflated state emission records let firms earn "paper credits" without cutting pollution, and environmentalists fought credits for closing plants. The review blamed state implementation, not trading itself {{cite:hahnhester1989}}.

As EPA phased lead out of gasoline, it let refiners trade and bank lead credits; a 2003 review found this saved hundreds of millions of dollars, though it likely increased violations {{cite:newellrogers2003}}. A Harvard retrospective says the 1990 program was partly inspired by the belief that a cap and strong monitoring could fix the earlier programs' shortcomings {{cite:chan2012}}.

## From research to change

In 1988, Senators Timothy Wirth (D-Colorado) and John Heinz (R-Pennsylvania) created Project 88, directed by economist Robert Stavins, then a new assistant professor at Harvard's Kennedy School and a former Environmental Defense Fund (EDF) official {{cite:chan2012,voss2007-wp,web-hks-stavins}}. Its December 1988 report proposed a cap-and-trade program for SO2 {{cite:voss2007-wp,chan2012}}.

In November 1988, George Mitchell (D-Maine), from one of the states most affected by acid rain, was elected to replace Byrd as Majority Leader, and president-elect George H. W. Bush had pledged action on acid rain {{cite:chan2012,ss2012-nber}}. Incoming White House Counsel C. Boyden Gray brought Stavins and EDF president Fred Krupp to the White House {{cite:chan2012,bush1990}}.

EDF senior economist Daniel Dudek and staff attorney Joseph Goffman worked with the White House on the first allowance-trading proposal; Hahn and economist Richard Schmalensee of MIT, a Council of Economic Advisers member, championed market tools there {{cite:chan2012,web-mit-schmalensee}}. Other environmental groups and most Democrats opposed market-based policies, some judging "pay to pollute" morally bankrupt; Bush White House participants later said the program needed EDF's credibility to win congressional support {{cite:chan2012}}. At EPA, by one journalist's account, career official Brian McLean proposed letting the market run, with rigorous monitoring {{cite:conniff2009}}.

On June 12, 1989, Bush proposed cutting SO2 by 10 million tons by 2000, as Canada and environmental groups had urged, and letting utilities trade credits {{cite:bush1989,chan2012}}. To win over high-sulfur coal states, Congress added bonus allowances for plants that installed sulfur scrubbers {{cite:voss2007-wp,chan2012,ss2012-nber}}. The final bill passed by votes of 89 to 10 in the Senate and 401 to 25 in the House, and Bush signed it on November 15, 1990 {{cite:voteview-s636,house-roll525,bush1990}}.

The law's Title IV set a permanent cap that reached its final level, 8.95 million tons a year or about half power plants' 1980 emissions, in 2010 {{cite:caa-title-iv,epa-arp}}. Congress set the cap and allocations itself, and trades needed no prior approval, which helped keep trading costs low {{cite:caa-title-iv,ss2015-rff}}. Every unit had to monitor emissions continuously, excess emissions drew a $2,000-a-ton penalty, and compliance was nearly 100% {{cite:caa-title-iv,ss2012-nber}}. Allowances were given free, mostly by each plant's 1985 to 1987 fuel use, which helped build political support, and banking unused ones produced more than half the program's cost savings {{cite:ss2015-rff,joskow1998}}.

## The impact

Between 1990 and 2004, power plant SO2 fell 36%, from 15.9 million to 10.2 million tons, while electricity from coal rose 25% {{cite:ss2012-nber}}. Unrelated rail deregulation in 1976 and 1980 cut the cost of shipping low-sulfur coal east from Wyoming and Montana {{cite:ss2012-nber}}. Citing the MIT evaluation led by economist A. Denny Ellerman, Schmalensee and Stavins attribute about one-third of early reductions to rail deregulation and two-thirds to the program {{cite:ss2012-nber,ellerman2000}}.

Costs came in far below forecasts, mostly because of the flexible cap and cheaper coal rather than trading alone, and at least 15% and perhaps 90% below the prescriptive alternatives studied {{cite:ellerman2003,ss2012-nber}}. Trading itself saved less than a 2000 Resources for the Future (RFF) study projected: $700 million to $800 million a year in 2010 (1995 dollars) if plants traded at least cost, against a uniform emission-rate standard (one rate for every plant) {{cite:aldy2022,carlson2000}}.

A 2018 paper, by a 2022 review's account the only true after-the-fact study of both costs and benefits, estimated savings of at least $200 million in 2002 (1995 dollars), about 20% of compliance costs, against a uniform standard with the same total emissions {{cite:chan2018,aldy2022}}. Part of the gap reflects costly scrubbers that some utilities chose with state regulators, and part the study's 2002 benchmark, when banked allowances kept emissions well above the cap {{cite:aldy2022,chan2018}}.

Four studies cited by Schmalensee and Stavins project yearly benefits for the fully phased-in program of $59 billion to $116 billion, about 90% of them from fewer deaths and illnesses caused by fine sulfate particles, against costs of $0.5 billion to $2 billion (2000 dollars) {{cite:ss2012-nber}}. One of the four put yearly costs in 2010 nearer $3 billion {{cite:chestnutmills2005}}. These are benefits of the emission cuts, not of trading as such, and ecosystems have recovered more slowly than expected {{cite:ss2012-nber}}.

From 1993, Harvard public health studies of 8,111 and then 552,138 adults linked fine particles, including sulfates, to higher death rates {{cite:dockery1993,pope1995,ss2012-nber}}. A 2021 study that tracked deaths downwind of plants as they installed sulfur controls found adult deaths fell, with reductions growing over time {{cite:barreca2021}}.

Title IV's success helped inspire the larger European Union Emissions Trading System, launched in 2005 {{cite:chan2012,ec-euets}}. One study puts its effect at 3.8% of carbon dioxide emissions from 2008 to 2016, against a world without carbon markets; another found cuts of 14% to 16% at regulated French manufacturers from 2005 to 2012, relative to unregulated firms {{cite:bayer2020,colmer2025}}. By April 2026, 40 emissions trading systems covered about 26% of global greenhouse gas emissions {{cite:wb2026}}.

As of November 2026, Title IV remains law, and units in the program emitted 647,000 tons of SO2 in 2023, 96% below 1990, though EPA credits cuts since 2005 largely to pollution controls and the shift to natural gas {{cite:epa-progress-2023}}. The United States has no national carbon market: the Senate abandoned a House-passed cap-and-trade bill in July 2010, and the World Bank's 2026 survey maps only state and regional ones there {{cite:ss2012-nber,wb2026}}. California extended its market in 2025 through 2045, though an April 2025 executive order had told the Attorney General to act against state climate laws judged illegal, faulting California's caps {{cite:icap-ca-2025,eo14260}}.

## What is still debated

**How much credit trading deserves.** The RFF study's authors, economists Curtis Carlson, Dallas Burtraw, Maureen Cropper and Karen Palmer, estimated that most gains from trading went unrealized in 1995 and 1996 {{cite:carlson2000}}. Ellerman's MIT group countered that a reasonably efficient market had formed by mid-1994, granting that some savings went unrealized; its estimate of about $350 million in early Phase I savings, about half the observed cost of compliance, assumed nearly efficient choices {{cite:ellerman2003}}.

**Where the pollution went.** Yale environmental researchers David Henry and Robert Mendelsohn and Middlebury College economist Nicholas Muller estimate, with conservative values for deaths, that trades from 2000 to 2007 raised damages $0.8 billion to $1.1 billion a year above the initial allocation, against the $0.7 billion to $2.1 billion a year in lower cleanup costs widely credited to trading {{cite:henry2011}}. The 2018 cost study found 2002 damages $170 million (1995 dollars) lower than under a uniform standard, but $2.1 billion higher program-wide than with no trading, as allowances moved east to more populated areas {{cite:chan2018}}.

Schmalensee and Stavins write that the program "did not generate significant hot spots" (local pockets of high pollution), while granting, citing Muller's earlier work, that trading rules weighted by local damage "could have been welfare-improving" {{cite:ss2012-nber,muller2009}}. One study found that SO2 trading did not concentrate emissions in Black or Hispanic communities but did shift them into less-educated ones {{cite:ringquist2011}}.

In the first three years of California's carbon market, 52% of facilities raised in-state greenhouse gas emissions, and neighborhoods with increases had more residents of color and poor residents {{cite:cushing2018}}. A later study modeling how pollution spreads estimated that gaps between disadvantaged and other neighborhoods in fine particles and nitrogen oxides from facilities covered only by that market narrowed 6% to 10% a year from 2012 to 2017 {{cite:hcm2023}}. A 2025 reply, coauthored by an author of the earlier California study, argued that this result may misidentify which facilities the cap covered {{cite:ashpastor2025,cushing2018}}.

**Caps that cannot adjust.** The SO2 cap was chosen near the point where cuts became steeply costlier and to satisfy environmental groups and a campaign pledge, not from benefit estimates; Title IV gave EPA no power to tighten it {{cite:ss2012-nber}}. Defenders reply that this rigidity meant less litigation than usual {{cite:ss2015-rff}}; one benefit study judged deeper cuts warranted {{cite:chestnutmills2005}}.

When EPA's 2005 Clean Air Interstate Rule sought deeper cuts through interstate trading, a federal appeals court struck it down in July 2008, holding that EPA could not ignore where upwind pollution lands {{cite:ss2012-nber}}. The 2011 replacement allowed only limited trading between states, demand for allowances all but vanished, and EPA's March 2026 auction drew no bids {{cite:ss2012-nber,epa-auction-2026}}.

**Limits of this account.** Several sources come from insiders: Stavins coauthored the 2012 reflections, drawn from a Sloan Foundation-funded workshop of people who designed and passed the program {{cite:chan2012}}. We could not obtain the Project 88 report or compare the program with Europe's prescriptive SO2 rules. Critics and defenders overlap: Schmalensee coauthored the MIT evaluation and, with Stavins, retrospectives cited here; Cropper both cost studies and the 2022 review; and Muller all three damage studies {{cite:ellerman2000,ss2012-nber,carlson2000,chan2018,aldy2022,henry2011,muller2009}}.

## What this case teaches

Title IV's [operational details](/lessons/3-design-details) fixed flaws in EPA's earlier trading. A cap in tons, measured at every unit, answered the paper-credit complaint; dropping prior approval helped keep trading cheap.

The proposal was ready when a [policy window](/lessons/6-policy-windows) opened. Project 88 reported in December 1988, as a president pledged to act prepared to take office and an acid rain state's senator became Majority Leader.

[Intermediaries carried the research](/lessons/4-knowledge-brokers). Project 88 turned decades of economics into a proposal, EDF lent it credibility and White House economists shaped the plan, with no formal research partnership.

Research did [a different job at each stage](/lessons/8-different-roles): theory supplied the concept, evaluations showed what failed, and later studies measured costs, benefits and harms. Yet politics was decisive and rail deregulation lowered costs; epidemiologists documented the largest, unplanned benefits.
