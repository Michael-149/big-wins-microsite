## The problem

In the 1980s, the first cellular licenses took about 2 years each to award, and users got a patchwork of local owners still lacking complete roaming agreements 12 years later {{cite:fcc1997,mmw2008}}. The Federal Communications Commission (FCC) assigns the radio frequencies, or spectrum, that wireless services use, and for most of the century the government gave licenses away {{cite:fcc1997,milgrom2004,hazlett1998}}.

For the first 30 cellular licenses, awarded in comparative hearings where rivals argued their merits, the FCC received more than 200 applications, many well over 1,000 pages {{cite:fcc1997}}. Congress allowed lotteries in 1981, and after the FCC dropped applicant screening by 1987, "application mills" helped almost 400,000 firms apply; at an estimated $800 an application, the FCC reckoned, nearly $300 million went into them {{cite:fcc1997}}. One speculator turned $5 million of licenses into $34 million within 18 months "without building so much as an antenna" {{cite:fcc1997}}.

The idea of selling licenses was old. In 1959 Ronald Coase, an economist joining the University of Virginia and later a Nobel laureate, proposed pricing spectrum so it would be, as he later put it, "awarded to the highest bidder" {{cite:coase1959,coase-nobel-bio}}.

## The research

Selling hundreds of licenses at once is hard: values are uncertain, and a firm may want several that are worth more together {{cite:kva2020}}. Auction theory offered concepts, not a design. Stanford economist Robert Wilson showed why sensible bidders bid below their own estimate when an item is worth the same to all but no one knows how much: they fear the "winner's curse," winning because they overestimated it {{cite:wilson1977,nobel2020-press,nobel2020-pop,kva2020}}.

In 1982 Paul Milgrom, Wilson's former doctoral student, and Robert Weber, both economists then at Northwestern University, showed that under assumptions they set out, formats that let bidders learn each other's estimates temper that curse and raise the seller's expected revenue {{cite:milgromweber1982,kellogg-weber,gga2014,kva2020}}. Federal agencies, among them the National Science Foundation, funded Wilson's and Milgrom's research {{cite:gga2014}}.

The design reached the FCC largely through bidders. After Congress authorized auctions in 1993, major bidders hired economists, including Milgrom, Wilson and Preston McAfee, then at the University of Texas at Austin, to comment on the FCC's rules and advise on bidding; the FCC hired economist John McMillan of the University of California, San Diego {{cite:mmw2008,gga2014,mcmillan1994}}.

Milgrom and Wilson proposed a simultaneous multiple-round ascending auction: bidding on every license stays open until the end, and an activity rule stops bidders from holding back {{cite:milgrom2004,mmw2008}}. McAfee independently proposed a similar design; he and McMillan had argued, from work on government procurement, that bid discounts for designated groups (bidding credits) cost less than reserving items for them {{cite:milgrom2004,gga2014,mmw2008}}.

Theory went only so far: on how bidders could assemble efficient groups of licenses, "economic analysis provided little guidance," McAfee, McMillan and a coauthor write {{cite:mmw2008}}. Charles Plott, an experimental economist at the California Institute of Technology, tested candidate rules in the laboratory during the rulemaking and contributed to the FCC's bidding software {{cite:plott1997}}.

## From research to change

Inside the FCC, Evan Kwerel, an economist who joined in 1983, had proposed auctions in 1985 with Alex Felker of its Office of Plans and Policy {{cite:kwerel1985,pps2021-kwerel}}. Congress's budget committees had long sought revenue from licenses, but the commerce committees overseeing telecommunications blocked auctions; in 1989 John Dingell, chair of the House commerce committee, introduced a bill banning them {{cite:hazlett2011}}. By 1993, write Thomas Hazlett, an economist then at George Mason University and a former FCC chief economist, and two coauthors, lagging second-generation wireless policy, a newly unified government (one party holding the White House and Congress) and the lotteries' waste meant "the system was primed for reform" {{cite:hazlett2011,clemson-hazlett}}.

The Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act, signed by President Bill Clinton on August 10, 1993, authorized auctions for services that subscribers pay for, leaving out free broadcasting {{cite:obra1993,mmw2008}}. Its four goals were rapid service; competition and wide participation (including small businesses and firms owned by minorities and women); recovery for the public of part of the spectrum's value; and "efficient and intensive use." It barred public-interest findings based "on the expectation of Federal revenues" and gave the FCC 210 days to write rules {{cite:obra1993}}.

Kwerel, who led the FCC's design group, came to favor a simultaneous ascending auction "based in large part on a proposal by Robert Wilson and me and a similar proposal by Preston McAfee," Milgrom recounts {{cite:milgrom2004}}. Facing skepticism, FCC Chairman Reed Hundt consulted other staff and, with their endorsement, "decided to take the risk of adopting a new auction design" {{cite:milgrom2004}}.

In 2002 Kwerel and John Williams, of the same office, proposed auctions in which license holders could sell spectrum and keep the proceeds, an idea Milgrom credits them with conceiving {{cite:kwerel-williams2002,milgrom2020}}. The FCC's 2010 broadband plan, citing the paper, asked Congress for that authority, and a 2012 law granted it, so TV stations, whose digital licenses had been issued without auction, could be paid to free airwaves {{cite:nbp2010,crs-r47578,usc309j,kva2020}}.

By Milgrom's account, his company, Auctionomics, designed the process and software for this incentive auction with Stanford economists Ilya Segal and Jonathan Levin, Stanford's president since 2024, and computer scientist Kevin Leyton-Brown of the University of British Columbia {{cite:milgrom2020,levin-cv}}. Each TV station saw a falling price and could leave for good, so its best move was to stay in while the price exceeded its value {{cite:lbms2017}}.

## The impact

By the FCC's own count, average time from application to license fell from 720 days in cellular hearings and 412 in lotteries to 314 for auctions completed by mid-1997, though start and end points differ {{cite:fcc1997}}. Peter Cramton, a University of Maryland economist whom bidders had hired, reviewed six auctions from 1994 to 1996: "Similar items sold for similar prices and bidders successfully formed efficient aggregations of licenses" {{cite:cramton1997,cramton-schwartz2002,mmw2008}}.

The FCC's 100 auctions from fiscal 1994 through 2025 generated more than $233.5 billion in nominal dollars (not adjusted for inflation), at a program cost of less than 1.2% of revenue {{cite:fcc-fy2027-budget}}. About a third came from one 2020 to 2021 auction, and the total is revenue, not savings, including sums paid on to broadcasters {{cite:fcc-prs-a107,da17-314}}. The Nobel committee calls the 1994 format "the dominant design for spectrum sales worldwide," with versions used in countries including Canada, Germany, India and the United Kingdom, whose 2000 auction raised £22.5 billion {{cite:kva2020,binmore-klemperer2002}}. In 2020 Milgrom and Wilson won the Nobel memorial prize in economic sciences {{cite:nobel2020-press}}.

The incentive auction opened aiming to clear 126 megahertz (MHz, a measure of a band's width) of TV airwaves, but broadcasters asked far more than wireless firms bid, and it closed in 2017 at 84 MHz {{cite:lbms2017,da17-314}}. Broadcasters received $10.05 billion, and wireless firms bid $19.8 billion {{cite:da17-314}}.

The FCC's general auction authority lapsed on March 9, 2023, when Congress did not agree on an extension, and a 2025 budget law restored it through September 30, 2034 {{cite:crs-r47578,fcc-fy2027-budget,crs-r48862}}. Under a separate 2024 law, the FCC sold 200 licenses for $3.57 billion in gross proceeds in June 2026 {{cite:da25-1075,fcc-prs-a113}}. As of November 2026, an auction of 3,248 new licenses is scheduled to start on April 27, 2027 {{cite:da26-1219}}.

## What is still debated

Budget politics opened the door: Kwerel and Gregory Rosston, a Stanford economist and former FCC deputy chief economist, credit "changes in the budget and budgetary process" and spectrum's rising value outside broadcasting {{cite:kwerel-rosston2000,stanford-rosston}}. The 1993 statute, though, made revenue one of four goals {{cite:obra1993,kva2020}}.

Revenue may be the wrong test. Auctions changed which firm gets a license, not how much spectrum regulators release for each use {{cite:kwerel-williams2002}}, and Hazlett and economist Roberto Muñoz of Chile's Federico Santa María Technical University estimate that licensing more bandwidth and keeping markets competitive generally matter more than license sales {{cite:hazlett-munoz2009}}. The Nobel committee counts revenue as a gain, since taxes cost the economy an estimated 17 to 56 cents per dollar raised, but warns that maximizing revenue may be a short-sighted aim {{cite:kva2020}}.

Economists Jeremy Fox of the University of Michigan and Patrick Bajari of the University of Minnesota estimate that in the 1995 to 1996 auction of licenses reserved for smaller firms, four large regional licenses instead of 493 small ones would have raised allocative efficiency (how well licenses go to the firms valuing them most) by 48% {{cite:fox-bajari2013,fcc1997}}. The designers wanted bidders, not the FCC, to decide which licenses belonged together {{cite:mmw2008}}. Without package bids (one bid for a group of licenses), firms needing several licenses risked winning only some at high prices {{cite:kva2020,fr2007-auction73,milgrom2000}}.

Economist Paul Klemperer of Oxford University, a designer of the United Kingdom's auction, argues that ascending auctions like the FCC's are "particularly vulnerable" to collusion and to entry deterrence (scaring off would-be bidders), citing European fiascoes {{cite:klemperer2002,binmore-klemperer2002}}. The Nobel committee replies that such problems would presumably be worse under other ways of allocating licenses {{cite:kva2020}}.

Auction theorists warned of collusion in 1993, and the FCC adopted rules against it {{cite:fcc1997}}. Cramton and economist Jesse Schwartz of Vanderbilt University, whose work began as advice to the Justice Department, found that in the 1996 to 1997 auction a small fraction of bidders signaled rivals, for example by ending bids with a market's three-digit number; they won over 40% of the spectrum and paid significantly less {{cite:cramton-schwartz2002,fcc1997}}. In 1998 the Justice Department sued three bidders over coded bids, and each consented to a judgment barring such bids {{cite:doj-mercury1998,doj-21stcentury1998,doj-omnipoint1998}}. Bajari and Jungwon Yeo, also of Minnesota, find signs that later limits on bid amounts and hidden identities curbed tacit collusion (coordination without an explicit agreement) {{cite:bajari-yeo2009,fr2007-auction73}}.

In early 1997, nine small-business licensees paying in installments said they were struggling, blaming more competition and weaker financial markets; the FCC suspended payments to consider their proposals and, noting that installments seemed to make it both regulator and lender, stopped offering them that year {{cite:fcc1997,mmw2008}}. Nearly all the licenses sold in June 2026 had come back after the FCC found two 2014 winners ineligible for the $859 million in small-business discounts they expected on them {{cite:da25-1075}}.

After Supreme Court rulings in 1995 and 1996 raised doubts about preferences based on race and sex, the FCC dropped such preferences to avoid the "uncertainty and delay" of legal challenges {{cite:fcc1997}}. Yale law professor Ian Ayres and Cramton estimate that such credits in one early paging auction raised revenue by $45 million by increasing competition {{cite:ayres-cramton1996}}.

In 2020 political economist Glen Weyl, then at Microsoft, argued that the 2012 law, by treating broadcasters' licenses as theirs to sell, "effectively amounted to a mass privatization of a public resource," and that the auction did little to curb private equity funds that had bought stations ahead of it {{cite:weyl2020}}. Economists led by Ulrich Doraszelski of the University of Pennsylvania estimate from simulations that owners of several stations, by holding some out of the auction, increase payouts to stations by 13.5% to 42.4% {{cite:doraszelski2025}}. Milgrom replied that Congress's choice of an auction made large payments inevitable and that the authors Weyl cited put the funds' manipulation profits at about $100 million; he acknowledged that the funds' pressure killed an Auctionomics proposal meant to limit their market power {{cite:milgrom2020}}.

McAfee and McMillan, two of the advisers, and economist Simon Wilkie of the University of Southern California say academic economists "were generally unwilling to defend their clients' goals when these goals conflicted with the social interest" {{cite:mmw2008}}. Roanoke College economist Edward Nik-Khah, using FCC archives, argues that such first-hand accounts omit how firms' funding shaped economists' participation, and the Nobel committee notes that a designer's advice could serve private interests {{cite:nikkhah2008,kva2020}}. Our account leans on participants too: advisers and FCC staff wrote much of the 1993 to 1994 history {{cite:milgrom2004,mmw2008,kwerel-rosston2000}}. We are part of Stanford University, as are Milgrom, Wilson, Segal and Rosston, and Levin is its president {{cite:nobel2020-press,milgrom2020,stanford-rosston,levin-cv}}.

## What this case teaches

[Operational details](/lessons/3-design-details) shaped how auctions worked: the activity rule succeeded, while the license map, installments and coded bids showed details failing. Milgrom writes that license terms "can be even more important than the auction rules" {{cite:milgrom2004}}.

The research reached the FCC through [intermediaries](/lessons/4-knowledge-brokers). Kwerel, inside the agency, proposed auctions in 1985 and two-sided auctions in 2002, while most academic economists came as bidders' consultants.

Auctions came through a [policy window](/lessons/6-policy-windows), a 1993 budget law with a 210-day deadline, when theory and a staff proposal were ready. The 2023 lapse shows that windows can also close.

Research did [a different job at each stage](/lessons/8-different-roles): theory, design proposals, laboratory tests, evaluations, a collusion study and a new design. Congress decided to auction; FCC staff recommended and built the 1994 design, which Hundt adopted {{cite:obra1993,milgrom2004,mmw2008}}.
