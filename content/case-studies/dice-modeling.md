winStatement: "From 2010 to 2025, U.S. agencies counted the dollar value of cutting carbon dioxide in dozens of regulatory actions, using a social cost of carbon first estimated with three models, including William Nordhaus's DICE."
summary: "In 1992, Yale economist William Nordhaus published DICE, an open model that weighs the costs of cutting greenhouse gases against the damages of warming. After court rulings in 2007 and 2008, a White House-led working group ran it alongside two other models, PAGE and FUND, with inputs it chose, to set the first full government-wide social cost of carbon: the dollar damage from one more ton of carbon dioxide. Agencies used it in dozens of regulatory actions until federal use, which changed with each administration, largely ended in 2025; Washington and Colorado still require such values by law."

## The problem

Global surface temperature in 2011 to 2020 was about 1.09 degrees Celsius (°C) above 1850 to 1900, and the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) calls human influence on warming "unequivocal" {{cite:ipcc2021}}. The latest version of the model this case follows puts the loss at 3.1% of world output at 3 °C of warming; an alternative its authors offer puts it near 9% {{cite:barrage2024}}. U.S. federal cost-benefit analyses did not value carbon dioxide cuts before 2008, and in 2008 and 2009 agencies' values ran from $0 to $80 a ton (2006 or 2007 dollars) {{cite:greenstone2011,iwg2010}}.

## The research

Yale economist William Nordhaus began work on the question in 1974 at the International Institute for Applied Systems Analysis (IIASA) in Austria, where, with National Science Foundation and IIASA support, he modeled the least costly way to keep carbon dioxide below a limit {{cite:nobel-bio,iiasa1975}}. In Nordhaus's account, the model's implied price on emissions later resurfaced as the social cost of carbon {{cite:nordhaus-lecture2018}}: the damage from one more ton of carbon dioxide over the years it does harm, converted into today's dollars by discounting, which counts later harm for less {{cite:nobel2018-background}}.

Nordhaus published DICE, the Dynamic Integrated Climate-Economy model, in Science in 1992 {{cite:nordhaus1992}}. An integrated assessment model, it links economic, climate and damage equations so that the costs of cutting emissions and the harm avoided can be compared in dollars; its code has been open from the start {{cite:nobel2018-background,nordhaus-lecture2018}}. The paper found "a modest carbon tax" efficient and "rigid emissions- or climate-stabilization approaches" costly {{cite:nordhaus1992}}.

Others built separate models: PAGE (Policy Analysis of the Greenhouse Effect, 1991), by Chris Hope, a policy modeler now emeritus at the University of Cambridge, and FUND (Climate Framework for Uncertainty, Negotiation, and Distribution, early 1990s), by Richard Tol, an economist at the University of Sussex {{cite:iwg2010,web-hope,tol2018}}. Nordhaus kept releasing new versions of DICE, the latest, DICE-2023, built with Lint Barrage, an economist at ETH Zurich {{cite:nordhaus2017,barrage2024,web-barrage}}.

## From research to change

In 2007 the Supreme Court held in Massachusetts v. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) that greenhouse gases fit the Clean Air Act's definition of "air pollutant" {{cite:massvepa2007}}, and in 2009 EPA found that they endanger public health and welfare {{cite:epa-endangerment2009}}. A House-passed 2009 bill to cap emissions and trade allowances got no further than the Senate calendar {{cite:hr2454}}.

The Center for Biological Diversity, 11 states and others challenged a light-truck fuel economy rule from the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) {{cite:ca9-2008}}. In 2008, the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals held the rule arbitrary and "contrary to" the Energy Policy and Conservation Act for, among other things, its "failure to monetize the value of carbon emissions" {{cite:ca9-2008}}.

In 2009 the Council of Economic Advisers and the Office of Management and Budget convened a working group with EPA and five departments to set one government-wide value {{cite:iwg2010}}. Michael Greenstone, then the council's chief economist and now at the University of Chicago, co-led it with legal scholar Cass Sunstein, then head of the budget office's Office of Information and Regulatory Affairs and now at Harvard {{cite:web-greenstone,greenstone2017,web-sunstein,eo12866}}.

For the February 2010 values, government staff ran DICE, PAGE and FUND themselves, weighting them equally {{cite:iwg2010,aldystavins2020}}. They kept each model's other features but chose three inputs: a probability distribution for climate sensitivity (the long-run warming from doubled carbon dioxide), five growth and emissions scenarios from the Stanford Energy Modeling Forum, and fixed discount rates of 2.5, 3 and 5% {{cite:iwg2010}}. The central value was $21 a ton for 2010 emissions (2007 dollars, 3% discount rate) {{cite:iwg2010}}.

We found no record that Nordhaus worked on the estimate, and in a 2011 paper funded by the Department of Energy he wrote that it "uses three models and then adjusts them in an ad hoc manner" {{cite:nordhaus2011}}. Economists Joseph Aldy and Robert Stavins of the Harvard Kennedy School call the group's use of DICE the one significant case of his research shaping policy directly. They add that DICE-based estimates "precluded efforts to avoid monetizing" carbon cuts in major rules, but judge his indirect influence "the most important" {{cite:aldystavins2020}}. Aldy was a White House adviser in 2009 and 2010 {{cite:web-aldy}}.

In 2013 the group reran newer peer-reviewed versions of all three models {{cite:iwg2016}}. At its request, the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine reviewed the method {{cite:iwg2016}}. Their 2017 report recommended rebuilding it from separate modules, updated about every 5 years {{cite:iwg2021,nas2017}}.

## The impact

From 2010 to 2025, agencies used versions of the value in rule analyses, and by 2014 the Government Accountability Office counted "dozens of regulatory actions" using it {{cite:iwg2010,epa2023,eo14154,gao2014}}. For 2012 to 2016 model-year vehicle standards, EPA figures presented by working group members, including Greenstone, put projected climate benefits at $176.7 billion (present value over 2010 to 2050 at 3%, 2007 dollars). With private fuel savings left out, the climate benefits turned the standards' net benefits from minus $68.4 billion to plus $108.3 billion. The fuel savings, projected at over $1.5 trillion through 2050 on the assumption that buyers ignore them, "dwarf all other cost and benefit categories" {{cite:greenstone2011}}.

A 2015 study of 36 federal rules from 2008 to 2013 found little evidence that valuing carbon changed which policy agencies chose {{cite:hahnritz2015}}. A 2014 analysis coauthored by Aldy and Greenstone found the value could decide whether the proposed Clean Power Plan's climate benefits exceeded its costs {{cite:aldystavins2020}}. We found no study measuring money saved; these are valuations and projections.

No statute set the value, which the group issued under a 1993 executive order on cost-benefit analysis; some laws, such as the fuel economy law, require such analysis, and in 2016 an appeals court upheld the Energy Department's use of a value counting global damages {{cite:iwg2010,eo12866,iwg2021}}. In 2017, Executive Order 13783 disbanded the group and withdrew its estimates, saying analyses must rest on "the best available science and economics," and pointed agencies to guidance on domestic effects and discount rates {{cite:eo13783}}. Counting only U.S. damages cut the values about sevenfold, to $7 a ton for 2020 emissions at 3%, from $50 (2018 dollars) {{cite:gao2020}}.

Restored by Executive Order 13990 in 2021, the group reissued its 2016 values adjusted for inflation, $51 a ton for 2020 emissions at 3% (2020 dollars), but we found no record of the final values the order required by January 2022 {{cite:eo13990,iwg2021}}. In November 2023, EPA issued its own peer-reviewed estimate: $190 a ton for 2020 emissions at a 2.0% near-term discount rate, in 2020 dollars. It drew on newer models, among them the Greenhouse Gas Impact Value Estimator (GIVE) from the research group Resources for the Future (RFF), and no longer used DICE, FUND or PAGE {{cite:epa2023,rennert2022}}.

In January 2025, Executive Order 14154 disbanded the group again and withdrew its estimates, citing "logical deficiencies, a poor basis in empirical science, politicization, and the absence of a foundation in legislation" {{cite:eo14154}}. A second order directed the budget office to revoke its 2023 guidance, which set a 2.0% default discount rate, and restore its 2003 version {{cite:eo14192,omb-a4-2023}}. May 2025 budget office guidance expects dollar values for greenhouse gases in "few to none" cases, as the uncertainties "are too great" {{cite:omb-m2527}}.

In February 2026, EPA rescinded its 2009 endangerment finding and repealed all vehicle greenhouse gas standards, saying the Clean Air Act does not authorize such standards "in response to global climate change concerns" {{cite:epa-2026}}. In September, NHTSA valued carbon at zero in its central fuel economy analysis, saying the 2008 ruling does not require otherwise {{cite:nhtsa-2026}}.

Washington's 2019 law requires electric utilities to use the 2016 federal values at a 2.5% discount rate in resource plans {{cite:wa-rcw-80-28-405,wa-rcw-19-280-030}}, Colorado's utility commission must use at least $68 per short ton (2,000 pounds) from 2020 {{cite:co-crs-40-3-2-106}}, and from 2017 Illinois priced credits meant to support nuclear plants at the 2016 federal value {{cite:ipa2017}}. New York recommends EPA's 2023 values, and Canada uses those from EPA's 2022 draft {{cite:ny-dec-2025,eccc-scghg}}.

## What is still debated

Economists have argued over discount rates at least since the IPCC's 1995 assessment {{cite:ipcc1995-ch4}}. The U.K. Treasury's 2006 Stern Review, led by economist Nicholas Stern, now at the London School of Economics (LSE), used a pure rate of time preference (the discount applied only because harm comes later) of 0.1% a year {{cite:lse-stern,web-stern,nobel2018-background}}. Nordhaus, using DICE, replied that its case for "sharp and immediate reductions" depends "decisively on the assumption of a near-zero time discount rate combined with a specific utility function" {{cite:nordhaus2007}}. Stern and Simon Dietz, a professor of environmental policy at the LSE, answered that the case for action rests on "the severe risks that the science now identifies" and on ethics {{cite:dietzstern2008,web-dietz}}.

Harvard economist Martin Weitzman argued that the consequences of a small chance of extreme warming and damage "can readily outweigh the effects of discounting" {{cite:weitzman2009}}. Nordhaus replied that such tail events (rare, extreme outcomes) "require careful analysis and attention" but there is "no universal rule" for when cost-benefit analysis should not apply {{cite:nordhaus2011tails}}. Economist Robert Pindyck of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology called integrated assessment models "close to useless as tools for policy analysis" {{cite:pindyck2013}}; his alternative, an expert survey, gave an average social cost of carbon of $200 a ton or more, or $80 to $100 after trimming outliers {{cite:pindyck2019}}.

A meta-analysis (a study pooling earlier estimates) by Peter Howard, then economics director at New York University's Institute for Policy Integrity, and Thomas Sterner, an environmental economist at the University of Gothenburg, found that their damage estimate, in place of DICE's 2013 one, raised the 2015 social cost of carbon three- to four-fold {{cite:howardsterner2017,howardsterner2016}}. A 2020 study coauthored by Sterner found the 2 °C target could be optimal in an updated DICE for most experts' views on discounting {{cite:hansel2020}}. In a 2021 study, Dietz and colleagues found that economic models delay warming too long after emissions, which pushes carbon prices too low {{cite:dietz2021dynamics}}.

DICE-2023 raised its damages at 3 °C from 2.1% to 3.1% of output, added a new carbon-cycle module (how carbon moves among the air, oceans and land) and put its cost-benefit optimum at 2.6 °C of warming by 2100 (from 1765); the 2 °C target passes its test with 2% discounting or Howard and Sterner's damages {{cite:nordhaus2017,barrage2024}}. The authors say its $66 a ton for 2020 emissions "aligns closely with other recent estimates" once discounting is considered. Like EPA, DICE starts from a real (inflation-adjusted) interest rate near 2%, then adds a risk premium on the view that climate investments share some market risk, for 4.5% at first; at a constant 2%, it gives $176 a ton {{cite:barrage2024,epa2023}}.

A broader critique rejects pricing carbon from damage estimates at all. Stern and two coauthors say the models' methodology "rests on flawed foundations" {{cite:sternstiglitz2022}}, another paper proposes prices set to reach net zero emissions {{cite:kaufman2020}}, and the U.K. has used such "target-consistent" values since 2009 {{cite:uk-desnz}}. Aldy, Stavins and two coauthors call a shift away from the social cost of carbon "ill advised" {{cite:aldy2021}}.

Counting only U.S. damages is also contested. A 2021 review coauthored by Aldy said a domestic focus "may be consistent with standard practice" but "ignores the externality that a country imposes on others" {{cite:aldy2021arre}}. In 2011, Nordhaus called the federal choice of global damages, made "without much justification," "a serious conceptual and potentially also a legal issue" {{cite:nordhaus2011}}.

Ties run across these estimates. Greenstone co-directs the Climate Impact Lab, which built one of EPA's three 2023 damage functions (formulas linking warming to economic losses); Howard and Sterner's meta-analysis underlies another {{cite:web-greenstone,epa2023}}. Economist Billy Pizer, RFF's president and a GIVE coauthor, reviewed the DICE-2023 paper {{cite:web-pizer,rennert2022,barrage2024}}.

## What this case teaches

DICE was ready when a [policy window](/lessons/6-policy-windows) opened: published and open for 16 years, it was running inside government 18 months after the 2008 ruling. Courts and White House officials opened the window; peer-reviewed estimates made a value of zero hard to defend {{cite:aldystavins2020}}.

Nordhaus fits the first of [two paths](/lessons/2-archetypes), a persistent researcher with a usable tool. He worked on the question for five decades, and because the code was public, government staff could rerun it with their own assumptions, with no research partnership we could find.

Change took [decades](/lessons/7-long-timeline): 35 years from the 1975 IIASA paper to the first full U.S. government-wide value in 2010. The National Science Foundation supported the early work.

The federal story shows how [changes outside permanent law were easier to undo](/lessons/5-legitimacy-narrative). With no statute setting it, the value changed with each administration, though an appeals court upheld its use and the group sought outside review; Washington and Colorado wrote such values into state law.
