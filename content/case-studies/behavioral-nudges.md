## The problem

In the two decades to 2007, US workplace coverage shifted from traditional pensions, typically employer-funded, to defined contribution plans such as 401(k)s, in which employees decide how much to save {{cite:crs2007-ae,thaler2004}}. At the end of 2000, 401(k) plans had more than 42 million active participants, and by 2005, 42% of private-sector workers were in a defined contribution plan, against 21% in a traditional pension {{cite:choi2002,crs2007-ae}}.

In the mid-1990s, joining a 401(k) plan usually meant filling in forms, choosing a savings rate and picking investments {{cite:nobel2017}}. At one large health care and insurance company, only 37% of those hired just before an April 1998 rule change had joined 3 to 15 months after starting {{cite:madrian2000wp}}. In Great Britain in April 2012, months before employers began enrolling workers automatically, only 42% of eligible private-sector employees saved in a workplace pension {{cite:dwp-2026-stats,dwp-2017-review}}.

## The research

In 1994, Richard Thaler, an economist who joined the University of Chicago in 1995 and became a Nobel laureate, proposed that 401(k) plans enroll employees by default {{cite:nobel2017,web-thaler-nobel,web-thaler-booth}}. By the late 1990s, employers were adopting it, many because their plans kept failing Internal Revenue Service (IRS) nondiscrimination rules, which penalize plans when lower-paid staff stay out {{cite:choi2002}}. In June 1998 the IRS ruled that a default contribution for new hires, with notice and a chance to take cash instead, counts as the employee's own contribution {{cite:irs-rr98-30}}.

Brigitte Madrian, then a University of Chicago economist and now dean of Brigham Young University's Marriott School of Business, studied the company's switch with Dennis Shea, a corporate compensation executive {{cite:treasury2000,byu-2026,madrian2000wp}}. From April 1998 it enrolled new hires at 3% of pay in a money market fund, a low-risk, low-return investment, unless they opted out {{cite:madrian2000wp}}. Hewitt Associates, a benefits consultancy, helped provide data; the National Institute on Aging supported the work {{cite:madrian2000wp}}. They found 86% of those hired after the switch in the plan, against the 37% hired just before, at equal time on the job; the participation gap between the lowest- and highest-paid shrank from about 55 percentage points to 15 {{cite:madrian2001,madrian2000wp}}.

Their paper also exposed a catch: of those hired under automatic enrollment, 61% stayed at both the 3% default and the money market fund, against 1% of earlier hires {{cite:madrian2000wp}}. By 2007, 3% was the most common default, partly because IRS examples used it, and employers, fearing liability, chose low-risk funds {{cite:crs2007-ae}}.

With economists James Choi, now at the Yale School of Management, David Laibson of Harvard and Andrew Metrick, then at the Wharton School, Madrian found participation above 85% at three companies {{cite:web-choi,choi2004}}. But low defaults and cautious funds roughly offset the gain in average saving, though saving rose among the lowest savers {{cite:choi2004}}.

Thaler and Shlomo Benartzi, a behavioral economist now emeritus at the University of California, Los Angeles, designed Save More Tomorrow, in which employees commit in advance to saving part of future raises {{cite:thaler2004,web-benartzi}}. Among 162 employees who chose to join at a midsize manufacturer, average saving rates rose from 3.5% to 13.6% over 40 months, with no randomized control group; Choi and colleagues cautioned that lower saving elsewhere could offset the gains {{cite:choi2002,thaler2004}}.

## From research to change

Treasury's July 2000 fact sheet promoting automatic enrollment cited the default studies, and Secretary Lawrence Summers publicly urged employers to adopt it {{cite:treasury2000,choi2002}}. J. Mark Iwry, Treasury's benefits tax counsel from 1995 to 2001, "formulated and directed" Treasury's strategy to approve and promote it, his Brookings biography says {{cite:web-iwry}}.

In 2005, Iwry and Brookings Institution economists William Gale and Peter Orszag proposed an "automatic 401(k)" for the Retirement Security Project, which The Pew Charitable Trusts supported {{cite:rsp2005}}. Their brief credited the default and Save More Tomorrow studies and asked Congress to confirm that federal law overrides restrictive state laws {{cite:rsp2005}}.

Congress acted in 2006, in its broadest pension overhaul since 1974, made urgent by underfunded traditional pensions {{cite:crs2007-ppa}}. The Pension Protection Act treated plans as passing nondiscrimination tests (a "safe harbor") if they enrolled workers at a default of at least 3% of pay rising to at least 6%, with a required employer contribution {{cite:ppa2006,crs2007-ppa}}. It also overrode restrictive state laws and shielded employers from liability for approved default investments {{cite:ppa2006,crs2007-ppa}}. The Nobel committee wrote that Save More Tomorrow "was important for" the Act, and Iwry's biography says he was "instrumental in developing" its automatic enrollment provisions {{cite:nobel2017,web-iwry}}.

An IRS letter in 2004 allowed defaults above 3% that rise on a schedule (automatic escalation), and a 2007 Labor Department rule protected employers who put defaulted savings in funds such as target-date funds, which grow more conservative with age {{cite:crs2007-ae,dol2007-qdia}}. Money market funds generally did not qualify {{cite:dol2007-qdia}}.

In Britain, a Pensions Commission led by Adair Turner, former head of the Confederation of British Industry, concluded that voluntary measures alone would not reverse falling private pension saving {{cite:drake2017,ukparl-turner}}. Its 2004 and 2005 reports recommended a duty on employers to enroll workers automatically, with contributions totaling 8% on a band of earnings, plus a low-cost national scheme {{cite:drake2017,dwp-2010-review}}. The Pensions Act 2008 set the core design, and the scheme became Nest {{cite:dwp-2017-review,dwp-2025-pc}}. A 2010 review, not asked to reconsider the policy, confirmed its design, citing UK employer surveys, New Zealand's experience and US studies such as Madrian and Shea's {{cite:dwp-2010-review,dwp-2017-review}}.

## The impact

In Britain, 9 million people had been automatically enrolled by 2017 {{cite:dwp-2017-review}}. The share of eligible private-sector employees saving in a workplace pension rose from 42% in 2012 to 88% in 2025 {{cite:dwp-2026-stats}}. Annual saving for eligible savers reached £166.1 billion in 2025, £63.5 billion more than in 2012 in real terms, 12% of it tax relief {{cite:dwp-2026-stats}}. These trends are descriptive, but Jonathan Cribb and Carl Emmerson, economists at the Institute for Fiscal Studies, used the roll-out by employer size to estimate the duty's effect: "substantial increases in pension participation and a rise in pension saving" {{cite:cribb2020}}.

In 2025 the government revived the Pensions Commission, calling automatic enrollment "a policy triumph" but noting that median contributions to private-sector defined contribution pensions had stayed at 8% of pay since 2019 {{cite:dwp-2025-pc}}. It projected that, as income from traditional pensions fades, someone retiring in 2050 will have 8% less private pension income than a 2025 retiree {{cite:dwp-2025-pc}}.

Among US plans Vanguard administers (not a national sample), the share with automatic enrollment rose from 10% in 2006 to 61% in 2025 {{cite:vanguard-has2026}}. Hardship withdrawals, where allowed, rose from 2% of Vanguard participants in 2021 to 6% in 2025 {{cite:vanguard-has2026}}.

The SECURE 2.0 Act of 2022, first proposed in 2020 by Representatives Richard Neal and Kevin Brady, requires new 401(k) plans, and 403(b) plans for school and charity staff {{cite:treasury2000}}, to enroll eligible employees at 3% to 10% of pay, rising to at least 10% {{cite:hr8696,fr-2026-414a}}. It applies from 2025 to plans set up from December 29, 2022, with exemptions including the newest and smallest firms; final rules issued on November 17, 2026, apply from 2028 {{cite:fr-2026-414a,treasury-2026}}. Treasury projects $4.2 billion a year on average in added retirement saving from 2023 to 2032, for about 2.2 million taxpayers a year, including $1.5 billion of tax subsidy and $0.6 billion moved from other saving {{cite:fr-2026-414a}}. For workers whose employers offer no plan, 15 state "auto-IRA" programs, which enroll them in individual retirement accounts, were open to all eligible employers as of October 31, 2026; such programs held $3.41 billion in more than 1.45 million funded accounts {{cite:chalmers2021,cri-2026}}.

Research did not start the practice or win its first approval {{cite:choi2002,irs-rr98-30}}. It measured the default effect and for whom it worked, showed the cost of low defaults, designed automatic escalation and supplied evidence that Treasury, the Retirement Security Project and Britain's 2010 review cited {{cite:treasury2000,rsp2005,dwp-2010-review}}.

## What is still debated

**Participation versus lifetime saving.** In 2025, MIT economist Taha Choukhmane, using US and UK data, found that automatic enrollment's gains shrink between 12 and 36 months after hire, when "the average savings increases are modest", though it "significantly lowers inequality in savings" {{cite:choukhmane2025}}. James Choi was on his dissertation committee {{cite:choukhmane2019}}.

Madrian, Choi, Laibson and colleagues, who disclose grants from the TIAA Institute and Nest, kept testing defaults {{cite:beshears2022}}. Among Army civilian hires, automatic enrollment in the federal Thrift Savings Plan from 2010 raised contributions over 4 years by 4.1% of annual salary, with "little evidence of increased financial distress" {{cite:beshears2022}}. But that finding leaves out car loans and first mortgages, and the authors could not observe assets outside the plan or measure net worth {{cite:beshears2022}}. In a plan that matched only contributions above its 12% default, just 25% of employees stayed at the default after a year, against about half at lower defaults, and stayers had lower incomes {{cite:beshears2025}}.

**Defaults versus lighter nudges.** Economist Stefano DellaVigna of the University of California, Berkeley, and public policy scholar Elizabeth Linos of the Harvard Kennedy School analyzed 126 randomized trials (which assign people to groups by chance) run by two US "nudge units", teams applying behavioral science for governments {{cite:dellavigna2022}}. Both units supported the study: the federal Office of Evaluation Sciences, still running in 2026, and the North American office of Britain's Behavioural Insights Team, where Linos had led research {{cite:dellavigna2022,oes-2026,web-linos}}. Most tested letters, reminders and simpler forms; the one that changed a default was set aside {{cite:dellavigna2022}}. The average nudge raised take-up (the share who acted) by 1.4 percentage points, against 8.7 points in academic journals, mostly because of selective publication; the authors still call the effect "sizable and highly statistically significant" {{cite:dellavigna2022}}.

Psychologist Maximilian Maier of University College London and colleagues reanalyzed a 2022 meta-analysis of nudges (a pooled analysis of many studies), correcting for publication bias, the tendency of weak results to go unpublished {{cite:mertens2022,maier2022}}. They found that "no evidence for the effectiveness of nudges remains", with evidence against an average effect on financial decisions; for structural changes such as defaults, the evidence was "undecided" {{cite:maier2022}}. The meta-analysis authors agreed the bias is "problematic" {{cite:mertens2022reply}}. A separate meta-analysis of 58 default studies found a considerable average effect but wide variation, including two negative results {{cite:jachimowicz2019}}.

**Where defaults reach less.** In OregonSaves, Oregon's state auto-IRA, 34.3% of covered workers had a balance in April 2020, and the most common reason for opting out was "I can't afford to save at this time" {{cite:chalmers2021}}. The authors find that the program "has meaningfully increased employee savings", with participation comparable to what automatic enrollment adds at larger private firms {{cite:chalmers2021}}.

**Who should set the default.** Economist Robert Sugden of the University of East Anglia and coauthors object that steering choices departs from people's own judgment of their interests {{cite:nobel2017,web-sugden}}. In their 2008 book *Nudge*, Thaler and legal scholar Cass Sunstein, now at Harvard, back only nudges that leave people better off "as judged by themselves", with opt-out open {{cite:nobel2017,web-sunstein}}. Stanford economist B. Douglas Bernheim and coauthors showed that the best default depends on why people keep it, with fairly consistent support for either the employer's highest matched rate or zero {{cite:bernheim2015}}.

**Structural reform.** Behavioral scientists Nick Chater of Warwick Business School and George Loewenstein of Carnegie Mellon University argued in 2023 that favoring individual-level fixes over system-level reform, an approach they had long backed, had led behavioral policy astray {{cite:chater2023}}. They call automatic enrollment and escalation "among the most ingenious and elegant" such fixes, yet "their impact has been disappointing", citing withdrawals, workers without plans and low defaults {{cite:chater2023aam}}. By their account, Laibson, a coauthor of several default studies above, concluded in a 2020 keynote that neither had moved the needle on retirement saving {{cite:chater2023aam}}. They grant that Thaler and Sunstein "rightly stress" that pension reforms combine both kinds of change {{cite:chater2023aam}}.

**Limits of this account.** We drew on published research and official records, not employers' internal histories. The part benefits consultants such as Hewitt played in spreading the practice appears in them only in passing {{cite:choi2002}}.

## What this case teaches

[Design details](/lessons/3-design-details) shaped what automatic enrollment achieved. At the first firm studied, a 3% default in a money market fund more than doubled participation but held most savers there. Regulators then allowed rising rates, Congress rewarded them in 2006 and a 2007 rule steered default money toward target-date funds.

Research did [a different job at each stage](/lessons/8-different-roles). Thaler proposed the idea, employers and the IRS acted first, and studies measured the effect, exposed its costs and designed fixes that legislators and regulators adopted {{cite:nobel2017,dol2007-qdia}}.

[Intermediaries](/lessons/4-knowledge-brokers) carried the evidence to rule makers. Iwry promoted automatic enrollment at Treasury, then helped turn the studies into the "automatic 401(k)" proposal. Britain's Pensions Commission turned evidence on undersaving into the employer duty and a national scheme.
