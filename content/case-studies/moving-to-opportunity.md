## The problem

In the mid-1990s, the families who signed up for Moving to Opportunity (MTO) lived in public and subsidized housing in neighborhoods (census tracts) where at least 40% of residents were poor, in Baltimore, Boston, Chicago, Los Angeles and New York {{cite:hud-mto-interim}}. Almost every household was headed by a woman, nearly two-thirds were African American, and three-quarters of household heads were on welfare. In more than 40% of households, someone had been a crime victim in the previous 6 months {{cite:hud-mto-final}}.

By one 2012 estimate, nearly 9 million Americans lived in extreme-poverty neighborhoods {{cite:ludwig2012}}. Rental assistance programs of the US Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD) serve nearly 5 million families {{cite:crs-fy27}}.

In the Gautreaux case, filed in 1966 by Chicago public housing residents against HUD and the Chicago Housing Authority, a court-ordered remedy let about 7,100 Black families move across the region from the late 1970s. Studies from the late 1980s suggested that families who moved to the suburbs did better, but the gains might reflect which families ended up there, a problem called selection bias {{cite:hud-mto-interim}}.

MTO was designed to test whether neighborhoods themselves shape people's lives {{cite:hud-mto-interim}}. Congress authorized it in 1992 and asked HUD to report on families' "long-term housing, employment, and educational achievements" {{cite:hud-mto-final}}.

## The research

From 1994 to 1998, MTO enrolled 4,604 families and assigned them by lottery to three groups {{cite:hud-mto-final}}. One, the experimental group, received housing vouchers, which pay part of the rent on a private apartment, plus counseling and help finding a unit; for the first year, they worked only in tracts where fewer than 10% of residents were poor. A second received regular Section 8 vouchers, and a control group kept its existing assistance {{cite:hud-mto-interim}}. Because a lottery assigned the offers, later differences between groups can be credited to them: the logic of a randomized controlled trial.

Of the experimental group, 48% moved with the voucher {{cite:hud-mto-final}}. After 4 to 7 years, HUD's interim evaluation found safer neighborhoods and better mental health for adults and girls, but no change in adults' employment or earnings {{cite:hud-mto-interim,kling2007}}. The final evaluation, directed by Jens Ludwig, a University of Chicago economist, with Harvard economist Lawrence Katz as principal investigator {{cite:hud-mto-final,web-ludwig,web-oi-team}}, found lower rates of extreme obesity, diabetes and major depression among adults 10 to 15 years on, but still no effect on their earnings or children's test scores {{cite:hud-mto-final,ludwig2011}}.

In the mid-2010s, Katz and two other economists, Raj Chetty (now at Harvard) and Nathaniel Hendren (now at MIT) {{cite:web-chetty,web-hendren}}, linked MTO records to tax data {{cite:chetty2016}}. Children whose families used the experimental voucher to move before age 13 earned 31% more in their mid-twenties than children in the control group: $3,477 a year on a control average of $11,270. Counting every child under 13 whose family was offered that voucher, whether or not it moved, the gain was $1,624, about 14%. Children 13 or older at the move did no better and, if anything, worse {{cite:chetty2016}}.

The authors had predicted this pattern before re-analyzing the data, which makes chance a less likely explanation {{cite:chetty2016}}. In a study of more than 7 million families who moved, Chetty and Hendren found that each year in a better area closed about 4% of the gap between a child's adult outcomes and those of children raised there {{cite:chettyhendren2018}}.

Economists Peter Bergman of the University of Texas at Austin and Christopher Palmer of MIT, Johns Hopkins sociologist Stefanie DeLuca, and Chetty, Hendren and Katz then tested Creating Moves to Opportunity (CMTO), a program to help voucher families move to such areas. Their randomized trial, from 2018 to 2020, enrolled 712 families with vouchers and a child under 15 in Seattle and King County, whose housing authorities developed the services with the researchers. Funders included the Gates and William T. Grant foundations {{cite:bergman2024}}.

Housing navigators helped families learn about high-opportunity areas, prepare rental documents, deal with credit problems and find units and landlords; the program also offered landlords a damage fund and families help with deposits. With CMTO, 53% of families moved to high-opportunity areas (neighborhoods where children from low-income families have tended to do better as adults), against 15% in the control group. About 87% of both groups found a place to rent, so CMTO changed where families rented, not whether {{cite:bergman2024}}.

In a second phase, full services had five times the effect of information plus financial incentives. Interviews by DeLuca's team suggested why: the program addressed each family's specific needs, "from identifying suitable units to providing emotional support to brokering with landlords" {{cite:bergman2023wp}}. Many low-income families, the authors conclude, do not strongly prefer to stay in low-opportunity areas {{cite:bergman2024}}.

## From research to change

HUD's fiscal 2017 budget request sought $15 million for a voucher mobility demonstration, up to $3 million of it for an evaluation {{cite:hud-cj-fy17}}. In a July 2018 House debate on a bipartisan bill for a similar demonstration, Rep. Bill Huizenga (R-Mich.) cited the 2016 paper's projected lifetime earnings gains, and Rep. Emanuel Cleaver (D-Mo.), its lead Democratic sponsor, urged colleagues to read the research {{cite:crec-2018,hr5793-actions}}.

The bill passed the House 368 to 19 {{cite:house-roll314}} but went no further in the Senate {{cite:hr5793-actions}}. Congress instead created the demonstration in its fiscal 2019 spending law and, with the fiscal 2020 law, provided $50 million for services and vouchers, plus $3 million for an evaluation {{cite:fr-2020-ccd,fr-2026-ccd}}. HUD's 2020 notice implementing it cites the MTO research and CMTO's August 2019 results and says HUD will draw on CMTO's experience {{cite:fr-2020-ccd}}.

## The impact

In April 2021, HUD awarded $45.7 million to nine lead and four partner housing agencies {{cite:hud-2021-awards}}. Early results for the first 596 families pointed the same way as CMTO: within 12 months, about 24% of families offered comprehensive services had moved to an opportunity area, against 4% of controls {{cite:hud-rce-2024}}.

By August 2024, sites had enrolled only about 3,200 families, 67% of the target for that point, and several agencies had withdrawn {{cite:fr-2026-ccd}}; 285 families offered comprehensive services had leased in an opportunity area {{cite:hud-rce-2024}}. In early 2025, concerned that a cheaper version of its services could weaken the main one and strain sites already struggling to enroll families, HUD dropped plans to test it. Testing one version needed fewer families, so HUD ended study enrollment early, on June 30, 2026 {{cite:fr-2026-ccd}}.

Services continue to October 1, 2028, including for new families outside the study {{cite:fr-2026-ccd}}. HUD expects its next report, on effects and costs, in 2026 {{cite:hud-ccd-eval}}; we found none published by November 2026.

Congress also provided $25 million in fiscal 2022 for services modeled on the demonstration's; in November 2023, HUD awarded $24.5 million of it to seven agencies outside the demonstration {{cite:hud-nofo-2023,hud-2023-mobility-awards,hud-2023-mobility-awardees}}. The Seattle Housing Authority said its $1,075,200 would help its CMTO program expand "beyond a pilot study" {{cite:sha-2023}}; the King County Housing Authority, whose earlier counseling pilot informed CMTO, now calls it a completed research partnership {{cite:kcha-mtw-2027}}. Proposals to fold vouchers and other rental aid into a smaller state block grant (fiscal 2026) and to stop reissuing vouchers when families leave (fiscal 2027) had not been adopted as of November 2026 {{cite:crs-fy26,nlihc-fy26,crs-fy27,govinfo-plaw-119}}.

## What is still debated

A 2021 review by economist Eric Chyn, then at Dartmouth College, and Katz, principal investigator of MTO's final evaluation and a CMTO coauthor, concludes that childhood neighborhoods affect long-run economic and educational outcomes; for adults, neighborhoods matter for health and well-being but have more ambiguous effects on labor market outcomes {{cite:chynkatz2021}}. The offer to move reduced arrests among girls; among boys it cut violent-crime arrests, at least in the short run, but raised property-crime arrests and problem behavior {{cite:kling2005}}. A 2014 JAMA paper finding more depression, post-traumatic stress disorder and conduct disorder among boys in voucher families was retracted and replaced in 2016 over errors in two tables; the boys' findings stand, but a reported decline in girls' depression does not {{cite:kessler2016,retractionwatch2016}}.

In a 2008 exchange, sociologists Susan Clampet-Lundquist of Saint Joseph's University and Douglas Massey of Princeton University argued that families' own choices within MTO limited what it could show, and found that time spent in different neighborhoods was associated with employment and earnings {{cite:clampet2008}}. Ludwig and other MTO evaluators replied that MTO had produced large changes in neighborhoods and that such estimates "add back the selection problems" the lottery was meant to overcome {{cite:ludwig2008,hud-mto-interim,hud-mto-final}}. Harvard sociologist Robert Sampson granted points to both sides but argued that segregated cities constrained where families ended up {{cite:sampson2008}}.

In September 2025, James Heckman, a Nobel laureate economist at the University of Chicago, and Sadegh Eshaghnia, a senior research associate in economics there {{cite:web-eshaghnia}}, argued in the Wall Street Journal that MTO "didn't improve employment outcomes for adults or educational outcomes for youths," and that studies of movers mistake family sorting for neighborhood effects {{cite:heckman2025}}. Chetty and six coauthors, including Katz and Chyn, accepted the point on adults but replied that children who moved young were more likely to attend college and earned more {{cite:oi-response-2025,chetty2016}}. They said they had tested studies of movers for sorting with sibling comparisons and placebo tests (a child's age at a move does not predict birth weight, which a neighborhood cannot affect), and agreed that claims of neighborhood destiny "can be overblown in public and advocacy circles" {{cite:oi-response-2025}}. The critics also cite an unpublished Danish study by Eshaghnia, which the two sides read differently {{cite:oi-response-2025}}.

Which neighborhoods count as high-opportunity is also uncertain. University of Chicago economist Magne Mogstad and coauthors show that tract and county mobility rankings become much less informative once statistical uncertainty is counted {{cite:mogstad2024}}; the CMTO authors reply that their program needs only the chosen areas to be better on average {{cite:bergman2024}}.

In HUD's eight-region demonstration, moves to opportunity areas rose less: about 20 percentage points within a year {{cite:hud-rce-2024}}, against about 38 in Seattle and King County {{cite:bergman2024}}. The comparison is rough: the demonstration mainly serves families who already hold vouchers and defines opportunity areas differently {{cite:hud-rce-2024,bergman2024}}. The CMTO authors had cautioned that Seattle's ban on discrimination against voucher holders, higher voucher rent limits in many neighborhoods and its agencies' high leasing rates could ease moves there, though its tight market could mean larger effects elsewhere {{cite:bergman2024}}.

CMTO's services cost $2,670 per family offered them, and moves to pricier areas raised voucher payments by an estimated $17,633 over 7 years per family that leased a unit. Counting both, and projecting the lifetime income of a child who moves at birth and grows up there, the authors estimate $1.35 in children's future income, discounted to present value, per $1 of government spending {{cite:bergman2024}}. HUD's evaluators put the demonstration's services at a preliminary $5,423 per family offered them, against $3,227 for CMTO's (its $2,670, in 2023 dollars), and call that similar to other mobility programs {{cite:hud-rce-2024}}; unlike CMTO, the demonstration includes help after the move {{cite:fr-2020-ccd}}.

The CMTO authors note that effects on the neighborhoods families leave and enter, especially at scale, need further study {{cite:bergman2024}}. CMTO's designers also evaluated it, though the research nonprofit MDRC {{cite:web-mdrc}} ran the trial under an analysis plan registered in advance {{cite:bergman2024}}. Abt Global and the Urban Institute evaluate HUD's demonstration, advised by DeLuca, Chetty, Katz, Sarah Oppenheimer (formerly research director at the King County Housing Authority) and others {{cite:hud-rce-2024,web-oppenheimer}}. We are part of Stanford University, where Chetty was on the faculty from 2015 to 2018 {{cite:harvard-gazette-2018b}}.

## What this case teaches

MTO showed that moving young children to lower-poverty areas could raise their later earnings, but not how to help families make those moves. CMTO's answer was a set of [operational details](/lessons/3-design-details) that together had five times the effect of information and money alone.

Research did [a different job at each stage](/lessons/8-different-roles): Gautreaux studies suggested the idea, the MTO lottery tested it, and tax records showed the payoff. CMTO, building on earlier mobility programs, then designed a service, and HUD's demonstration is testing a version of it in eight regions.

The payoff took [decades](/lessons/7-long-timeline). The earnings finding came more than 20 years after enrollment began; a funder judging MTO by its 2003 or 2011 reports would have seen health gains but no earnings gains.

In CMTO, researchers [worked closely with decision makers](/lessons/1-public-engagement): two housing authorities developed it with them. Interviews with families also helped explain why full support worked.
