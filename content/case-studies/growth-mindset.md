## The problem

Ninth graders in the United States have the lowest average grade point averages (GPAs) of any high school grade, and those who fail ninth-grade classes are far less likely to graduate {{cite:mdrc2019}}. More than 3 million ninth graders attend regular US public schools each year, and under the Every Student Succeeds Act of 2015, many states made ninth-grade poor performance a key measure of school accountability {{cite:yeager2019,pace2018}}. Promising secondary-school programs tested in federal Investing in Innovation trials typically trained teachers or changed curricula and cost several thousand dollars per student {{cite:yeager2019}}.

## The research

Carol Dweck, a psychologist now at Stanford University, has studied how children respond to failure since the early 1970s {{cite:dweck1973,dweck-profile}}. In 1988, Dweck and a coauthor proposed that beliefs about whether intelligence is fixed or can grow steer how people respond to difficulty {{cite:dweck1988}}. The belief that intellectual ability can be developed is now called a growth mindset {{cite:yeager-dweck2020}}.

In 1998, Dweck and a Columbia University colleague found that fifth graders praised for intelligence, rather than effort, persisted less and did worse after failure {{cite:mueller1998,dweck-profile}}. The first tests of teaching the belief itself came from researchers studying stereotype threat (the worry of confirming a negative stereotype about one's group), with African American college students in 2002 and adolescents in 2003 {{cite:aronson2002,good2003,burnette2023}}.

Psychologist Lisa Blackwell, then of Columbia University, reported with Dweck and a colleague in 2007 that seventh graders who believed intelligence can grow earned rising math grades. In a small trial, an eight-session workshop halted a decline in math grades for 48 students while 43 control students kept slipping {{cite:blackwell2007,yeager2019,yeager-dweck2020}}.

Brief online sessions came from PERTS, the Project for Education Research That Scales, a Stanford laboratory turned nonprofit. Dave Paunesku, who earned his Stanford doctorate under Dweck and Stanford psychologist Gregory Walton, co-founded it {{cite:paunesku-bio,walton-profile,perts-careers,perts-about}}. In a study of 1,594 students in 13 high schools, published in 2015, its online modules raised core-course grades for the third of students at risk of dropping out {{cite:paunesku2015}}.

The main test, the National Study of Learning Mindsets, was led by David Yeager, a University of Texas at Austin psychologist, with Walton, Dweck and others and published in Nature in 2019 {{cite:yeager-profile,yeager2019}}. An independent firm, ICF, drew a national sample of 139 public high schools; 76 took part and 65 provided records. In 2015 and 2016, ninth graders were randomly assigned, as in a lottery, to two online sessions of about 25 minutes, which taught that the brain grows stronger with hard learning, or to a lesson on brain functions; the analysis covers 12,490 of them {{cite:yeager2019}}.

The analysis plan was registered in advance, and a second research firm, MDRC, processed the grades without knowing how its choices would affect the results. The authors disclosed Paunesku's role at PERTS and that six authors, including Yeager, Dweck and Walton, had done paid or unpaid speaking or consulting; none had a financial relationship with any seller of growth mindset products {{cite:yeager2019}}.

For lower-achieving students, those at or below their school's median GPA beforehand, being assigned the program raised core-course GPAs at the end of ninth grade by 0.10 grade points, or 0.11 standard deviations (a unit for comparing effects across scales), by the authors' preregistered estimate. Higher achievers' grades did not change {{cite:yeager2019}}. Effects were larger in schools where peers welcomed challenge and smaller in higher-achieving schools; in an exploratory analysis (not preregistered), 10th-grade enrollment in advanced math rose from 33% to 36% {{cite:yeager2019}}.

MDRC's re-analysis, funded by the Gates Foundation, used slightly different samples, weights and controls and estimated 0.06 grade points for lower achievers and, like the authors, 0.05 for all students. It called its results "substantively consistent" with the authors' and the effect "on par with" other high school programs', at far lower cost {{cite:mdrc2019}}.

## From research to change

Dweck's 2006 book, Mindset, brought the idea to general readers {{cite:dweck2006}}. Mindset researchers, including Dweck, founded a for-profit company, Mindset Works, which put its paper-based Brainology curriculum online with a 2010 federal small-business research award {{cite:macnamara2023-preprint,barshay2022,ies-sbir}}. Released in 2012, the online version was "purchased by hundreds of schools" and marketed by Scholastic to districts {{cite:ies-sbir}}. PERTS made free videos for teachers with ClassDojo in 2016 and, from the 2017 to 2018 school year, offered US high schools a free program built from the national study and funded by Character Lab and the Bezos Family and Raikes foundations {{cite:classdojo2016,perts-hg,perts-faq}}.

Researchers from the World Bank's behavioral science team and Peru's [Group for the Analysis of Development](https://www.grade.org.pe/en/) worked with the Ministry of Education and its innovation lab, MineduLAB, on a 90-minute session based on the US studies {{cite:embed2018,outes-leon2020,jpal-minedulab}}. In 2018 the World Bank reported that the ministry was scaling it up {{cite:embed2018}}.

In a 2016 online poll of more than 600 teachers registered with edweek.org, 77% said they were familiar or very familiar with growth mindset, and the practice they most often reported was praising students for taking risks and persevering {{cite:edweek2016}}. California's 2023 Mathematics Framework, the state's guidance for math teaching, cites the national study and a favorable meta-analysis (a study pooling many studies' results) in arguing that changing students' mindsets raises their math achievement {{cite:ca-math-framework}}.

## The impact

The national study's authors project that the program would cut the share of lower-achieving students with a D or F average from 46% to 41%, which, applied nationally, would keep 5.3% of 1.5 million such students a year from being "off track" for graduation {{cite:yeager2019}}. MDRC estimated that the share of lower achievers with a GPA of 1.0 or below fell 3.3 percentage points {{cite:mdrc2019}}. As of November 2026 we found no published estimate of the program's effect on graduation {{cite:txbspi-nslm}}.

In Peru's 2015 trial, which sampled 800 public schools, math scores 2 months after the session were 0.05 standard deviations higher across all assigned schools, though only about 60% held it; the estimate was marginally significant (narrowly missing the usual test for ruling out chance). The gain was 0.13 in the regional sample outside metropolitan Lima and about zero inside it, and still about 0.10 there after 14 months {{cite:outes-leon2020}}. In Norway, an experiment in 49 of 50 academic-track high schools, reported in 2021 by economists with Yeager, Walton, Dweck and other national-study authors, replicated the rise in advanced math enrollment and found that schools' course policies affect whether students can benefit {{cite:rege2021}}.

In England, a small pilot of pupil workshops found an estimated 2 months' extra progress, not statistically significant. That "promising" result led to a larger Education Endowment Foundation trial in which teachers delivered the program in 101 primary schools; its 5,018 pupils, including those from low-income families, made no added progress in literacy or numeracy, although the program was "delivered as intended." The evaluators' explanations included the idea's "widespread use": over a third of comparison-school teachers had attended growth mindset training days {{cite:eef2019}}. In Argentina, a similar exercise for 12th graders in 202 Salta province schools was "implemented as intended" but had no measurable effect {{cite:ganimian2020}}.

The programs cost little: PERTS's was free to schools, Peru's averaged $0.20 per pupil and England's about £4 {{cite:perts-hg,outes-leon2020,eef2019}}. We found no documented savings; the only benefit-cost estimate we located, from the Washington State Institute for Public Policy, is a model projecting future earnings from test-score effects {{cite:wsipp2024}}.

PERTS discontinued Growth Mindset for 9th Graders in June 2025; its program page gives no reason {{cite:perts-hg}}. We found no reliable count of students who have taken these programs and no federal rule built on the research.

## What is still debated

In the evidence above, trials run or reported by the programs' developers found gains, mostly for struggling students; independent evaluations found smaller gains (MDRC, Peru) or none (England, Argentina). The developers reply that large studies, including those by third parties, "justify confidence in growth mindset research" {{cite:yeager-dweck2020}}.

In late 2022, two meta-analyses in Psychological Bulletin disagreed. Psychologists Brooke Macnamara, then of Case Western Reserve University and now of Purdue University, and Alexander Burgoyne, then of the Georgia Institute of Technology, whose 2018 review had found programs averaging 0.08 standard deviations, pooled 63 studies of 97,672 students. The average effect, 0.05, was not significant after correcting for possible publication bias (positive results' greater chance of being published); it was a nonsignificant 0.02 in the six highest-quality studies and 0.04 in the 13 where beliefs measurably changed, and none of the theory's moderators (factors that should change the effect's size) made a significant difference. They concluded that apparent effects "are likely attributable to inadequate study design, reporting flaws, and bias" {{cite:macnamara2023,cwru2022,purdue-slp,sisk2018,macnamara2023-preprint}}.

A team led by Jeni Burnette, a North Carolina State University psychologist whose group has run its own growth mindset trials, found 0.14 standard deviations for targeted students in well-delivered programs and predicted that 95% of similar future programs' effects would fall between -0.08 and 0.35 {{cite:burnette2023,barshay2022,burnette2018}}. Northwestern University statistician Elizabeth Tipton and colleagues re-analyzed Macnamara and Burgoyne's data and found meaningful effects for targeted groups; Tipton and three coauthors, including Yeager, were national-study authors {{cite:tipton2023,yeager2019,barshay2022}}. Macnamara and Burgoyne replied that Tipton's team "changed effect sizes, how moderators were coded, and which studies were included, often without explanation" {{cite:macnamara2023-reply}}. A 2025 review of 24 randomized trials by education researcher Carolina Gazmuri, then of Durham University, found null or very small effects in the strongest studies and advised against investing much time or money in the programs, though near-free use might be reasonable {{cite:gazmuri2025}}.

Macnamara and Burgoyne also report that authors with a known financial incentive, which they define as ties to a company or nonprofit that sells or promotes the programs, paid speaking or book profits, were "two and a half times as likely to report positive effects" {{cite:macnamara2023-reply,macnamara2023-preprint}}. Tipton's team objects that this coded conflicts by authors' later outside income rather than by each paper's disclosures, and finds no significant effect of conflicts when moderators are modeled jointly {{cite:tipton2023}}. Macnamara and Burgoyne answer that the team inconsistently recoded some studies' incentive status {{cite:macnamara2023-reply}}. Dweck said in 2022 that she divested from Mindset Works "years ago," when she realized it was a conflict of interest; Macnamara and Burgoyne count her speaking and book income as incentives {{cite:barshay2022,macnamara2023-reply}}.

Yeager and Dweck wrote in 2020 that interventions aimed at teachers rather than students "have not yet been effective" {{cite:yeager-dweck2020}}. Mindset Works' own federally funded trial of teacher-delivered Brainology in grades 6 and 7, led by Blackwell, reported higher grades for struggling students, most where teachers began with fixed mindsets; in the national study, by contrast, gains in math grades were concentrated among students whose math teachers held growth mindsets {{cite:porter2022,ies-brainology,ies-sbir,yeager2022}}. A close replication of the 1998 praise study with Chinese children found little support for it {{cite:li2019}}.

Dweck warned in 2015 of what a colleague called a "false growth mindset" among educators, and of praise for students "who are putting forth effort, but not learning." She also feared that using mindset measures for accountability would "create false growth mindsets on an unprecedented scale" {{cite:dweck2015}}.

We are part of Stanford University, as are Dweck and Walton; PERTS began as a Stanford laboratory, and Stanford helped fund the national study {{cite:dweck-profile,walton-profile,perts-careers,yeager2019}}. This account rests on published sources. We could read several papers, including the 2002, 2003 and 2007 trials, only as abstracts or through other papers' accounts.

## What this case teaches

Research reached students through [intermediaries](/lessons/4-knowledge-brokers). PERTS built free online sessions, World Bank and Peruvian researchers made a version with Peru's ministry, and Scholastic marketed Mindset Works' program to districts.

Growth mindset shows how [ideas spread in untested forms](/lessons/5-legitimacy-narrative): it was known to most teachers polled in 2016, 3 years before the national study was published, and what spread in classrooms, such as praise and teacher training, was not what that study tested. That study used a national sample, outside firms handling data, a preregistered plan and an independent re-analysis. England's trial of a teacher-delivered version found no gain.

Dweck fits the first of [two paths](/lessons/2-archetypes), a persistent researcher: she pursued the question from 1973 to the 2019 national study, wrote a popular book and co-founded a company. But a wider cast funded, built and tested the programs: PERTS, Yeager's team, research firms, foundations, federal agencies and a ministry abroad.

The work took [decades](/lessons/7-long-timeline), from the early 1970s to 2019, and drew on a federal small-business award, foundations and Stanford. It gave schools a free program of under an hour, with a small, contested benefit for lower achievers and no documented savings.
